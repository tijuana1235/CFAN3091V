Progress Reports

The Backbone of the Early Course

Why These Exist

A progress report is a short, honest account of where your project stands. It is the same structure I use with my graduate students, and it exists for one reason: you do not show up to a meeting empty-handed.

Two of these reports are the agenda for your individual meeting with me. If you have not written one, we spend the first five minutes of a fifteen-minute meeting reconstructing where you are, and that is five minutes you do not get back. If you have written one, I have read it before you sit down and we start at the actual problem.

The other reason is quieter. Writing down what you did, what stalled, and what you plan next is how you notice a project drifting while there is still time to correct it. Most thesis work fails slowly and in private. These reports make it public early.

Which Reports You Owe

For Week 3, use the dedicated Progress Report 1 and Thesis Progress Report 1 forms. For Week 7, use the Progress Report 3 and Thesis Progress Report 2 forms, where the Fall Graduation form carries the Professional Review Plan on its second page. Each page provides a printable form for each course track.

Each report is one of the 15 milestones on your track. Keep each report to one page. Bring meeting reports on paper and hand them in after the meeting. The Week 5 report includes a multi-part Search Record, so that milestone is a Canvas file upload.

The Template

Every report answers the same four questions. Keep it to one page. This is not an essay, and length is not the point.

1. What did you accomplish since the last report?

Be specific. Dates, meetings, hours, sources added, words drafted, analyses run. “I worked on my literature review” tells me nothing. “I added nine sources to Zotero, read four of them closely, and drafted two paragraphs on the nitrogen-timing disagreement” tells me where you are.

2. What did you struggle with, and how did you try to resolve it?

The second half of that question matters as much as the first. Naming an obstacle is useful. Naming what you already tried tells me whether you need a different approach or just more time, and it keeps me from suggesting the thing you did last Tuesday.

3. What is your plan for the next period?

Specific tasks with specific deadlines. “Keep reading” is not a plan. “Finish the three forward-citation papers by Oct 12 and draft the methods paragraph by Oct 15” is a plan, and it gives us something to check against next time.

4. What questions do you have for me?

Bring them written down. The questions you think of in the moment are rarely the ones that have been slowing you down for two weeks.

What Each Report Adds

The four questions are the same every time. What changes is the checkpoint around them. Each form opens by asking what actually moved since the last report, in the specific terms of that stretch of the course, and closes by naming the one decision that has to be settled before the next milestone. That opening and closing pair is what keeps three reports from being the same report written three times.

Progress Report 2 Has No Printable Form

Progress Report 2 is the one report in the series without one, because it uploads to Canvas with the Search Record rather than being handed in at a meeting. Its checkpoint questions belong inside the four standard questions:

  • which next actions from your Week 3 meeting you completed, and which slipped;
  • what changed when you rebuilt the question in Week 5, and what forced the change;
  • one search that failed or came back too broad, and what you changed; and
  • the published result you are bringing to Week 6, and why it matters to your project.

What Progress Means at Different Points

Progress is not one thing. Early in the term it looks like narrowing and contacting; late in the term it looks like drafting and revising. Judge yourself against the stage you are actually in.

Fall Graduation students should read those rows as thesis-completion work: the gating factor for completion, the alignment between finished work and the claims the thesis can support, and the analysis still outstanding.

What “Satisfactory” Means

Progress reports are a completion check-in. I read them; I do not give line-level feedback on them. See the feedback tiers.

A report is satisfactory when it:

  • answers all four questions;
  • is specific enough that someone could act on it, with named tasks, sources, dates, or obstacles rather than general statements of effort;
  • is submitted before the deadline, which for meeting weeks means before your meeting time, not after it; and
  • describes the work honestly, including the parts that did not go well.

A report is not yet satisfactory when it is missing a question, is written at a level of generality that could describe any student in any week, or arrives after the meeting it was supposed to structure.

NoteA Report Saying “I Am Stuck” Is Satisfactory

Satisfactory describes the report, not the project. A week where you accomplished little, struggled with something you could not resolve, and have questions you cannot answer yourself is a completely normal week in thesis work. Write that down without dressing it up and the report is satisfactory. What is not satisfactory is concealing it, because then I cannot help and we both discover the problem in November.

Search Record Attached to Progress Report 2

Progress Report 2 includes a compact search record. This is how you show that the source set was built through decisions rather than accumulated by chance. Record:

  • the research question or concepts that bounded the search;
  • each database, review article, or other starting point used;
  • the major terms, filters, and backward or forward citation chains tried;
  • at least one search that failed or was too broad, and what you changed;
  • why the current core sources were included;
  • sources excluded after inspection and the reason, when relevant; and
  • unresolved evidence or a discipline-specific database still to search.

Attach the annotated core-source table or Zotero export showing at least 15 purposefully selected sources and five substantive annotations. A source count alone is not satisfactory. The record must let a reader reconstruct the major search decisions and understand why the retained sources fit the question.

Any milestone rated not yet satisfactory may be revised and resubmitted once within one week. See the resubmission policy.

The Professional Review Plan

Fall Graduation students submit this with Thesis Progress Report 2 in Week 7. Proposal Development students submit it in Week 11 with Student Peer Review 2. It is short, and it names four things:

  1. Who will review your document: the Primary Advisor, and the Proposal Committee members if your committee is formed. Many spring and summer graduates will not have a full committee yet, and that is expected. Name who you have.
  2. When you will send it, which is after the Week 12 release screen and no later than Wednesday, Nov 25.
  3. When you expect feedback, which is around Dec 9.
  4. Confirmation that the people named know it is coming and have agreed to that window.

Point four is the one students skip, and it is the one that matters. Faculty calendars in early December are full. A reviewer who learns in late November that a proposal is arriving has already lost the time to read it well.
