Peer Review Guidelines

Giving and Receiving Feedback in CFAN 3091V

Why Peer Review?

Students in previous semesters consistently identified peer review as the most valuable component of this course:

“Most peer review in a course. Found the peer review extremely helpful.”

“Low-stakes environment of the editing. Doesn’t need to be perfect.”

Peer review builds two skills simultaneously: the ability to evaluate others’ work critically, and the ability to receive critique without defensiveness. Both are essential in academic and professional life.

Structure

You will complete two structured student peer reviews during the semester, in Weeks 9 and 11. Week 14 is a professional review by the Primary Advisor and/or Proposal Committee, not a third student peer review.

General Guidelines

As a Reviewer

  1. Read the full document before writing any comments
  2. Start with what works: identify specific strengths before addressing weaknesses
  3. Be specific: “This paragraph is unclear” is unhelpful. “The transition between your literature review and your research question on page 3 is abrupt: consider adding a sentence that connects Theme X to your specific gap” is actionable.
  4. Ask questions: “What would happen if…?” and “Have you considered…?” are more productive than “This is wrong.”
  5. Focus on the work, not the person: “The methods section needs more detail” not “You didn’t write enough.”

As a Receiver

  1. Read all feedback before reacting: your first instinct may be defensive; that’s normal
  2. Separate the signal from the noise: not every suggestion needs to be adopted, but every suggestion deserves consideration
  3. Ask for clarification if a comment is unclear
  4. Document your response: in your process portfolio, note what feedback you incorporated, what you declined, and why

Peer Review Worksheets

The stable worksheet criteria are published below, and paper copies will be available in class. Complete each review on paper and hand the worksheet and writer response to the instructor after the workshop. The result appears in the Canvas gradebook, but there is no routine Canvas upload. Each review requires you to point to evidence in the draft, explain why the issue matters, and name a priority. Three comments without that reasoning are not automatically three actionable suggestions.

Peer Review 1: Literature Synthesis

  1. State the writer’s research question and literature-based rationale in your own words. Mark where the draft made each one visible.
  2. Identify one paragraph that synthesizes multiple sources. Name the sources and explain what relationship the paragraph establishes among them.
  3. Identify one place that remains source-by-source summary, or explain why you found none.
  4. Test one consequential claim against its cited evidence. Is the wording no stronger than the evidence warrants?
  5. Identify a disagreement, limitation, or boundary the draft handles well and one that needs more work.
  6. Recommend the single highest-priority structural revision. Point to the relevant passage and explain what the revision would improve.

Peer Review 2: Question, Method, and Outcomes

  1. Trace the argument chain: research question, evidence-based opening, proposed method, expected or actual result logic, and significance. Mark the first place the chain breaks or becomes hard to follow.
  2. Test whether the unit, population, system, cases, or materials match the question.
  3. Identify one missing design or analysis detail that would keep a reviewer from judging feasibility or answerability.
  4. Check the applicable constraints: access, time, equipment, permissions, ethics, sample or case availability, and an alternative if the preferred design fails.
  5. For a proposal, test whether at least two plausible outcomes can be distinguished using the proposed evidence. For a completed thesis, test whether the claims remain within the actual results.
  6. Recommend the single highest-priority revision. Point to the relevant passage and explain why it comes before sentence-level editing.

After each review, the writer adds a short response naming one structural revision they will make, one recommendation they need to investigate, and one recommendation they will modify or decline with a reason. This response enters the process portfolio.

How Peer Review Is Evaluated

You are evaluated on the quality of the reviews you give, not on how well your own work is received:

  • Satisfactory: Every worksheet question is answered with evidence from the draft. The review identifies strengths and weaknesses, distinguishes a high-priority structural revision from minor editing, and explains why its recommendations would improve the question-evidence-method argument.
  • Not Yet Satisfactory: The review is generic, incomplete, unsupported by passages in the draft, or supplies edits without explaining their priority or purpose. It may be revised and resubmitted within one week.

Professional Review in Week 14

After the Week 12 release screen, send the revised proposal or thesis to the Primary Advisor and/or Proposal Committee. The Week 14 submission contains:

  • evidence that the document was sent and reviewed;
  • the comments, annotated draft, or meeting notes produced through the review;
  • a response memo explaining which major recommendations you will accept, modify, or decline; and
  • a prioritized revision plan for the applicable final deadline: Dec 14 for Fall Graduation students and Dec 16 for Proposal Development students.

You are evaluated on how you prepare for, document, and use professional feedback. Your grade does not depend on the reviewer’s writing style or the amount of feedback supplied.

A dated send record, your own summary of the substantive feedback, and your response are sufficient evidence. Redact personal contact information and anything unrelated to the project. Raw email threads and unredacted reviewer comments are optional and are not expected.
