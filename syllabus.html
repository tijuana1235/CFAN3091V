Syllabus: CFAN 3091V

From Ideas to Strategic Plans | Fall 2026

Published

January 1, 2026

Welcome to CFAN 3091V. This is an upper-level undergraduate course in the College of Food, Agricultural and Natural Resource Sciences (CFANS) that guides honors students through the research design, technical writing, and professional communication needed to develop a complete honors thesis proposal or, for students graduating that fall, complete the honors thesis.

Course Description

You have a great research idea, now what? How do you turn your idea into a proposal? It has been said, paraphrasing Edison, that innovation is one percent inspiration, 99 percent perspiration. In this course, we will start with an inspiring idea and sweat our way to develop a research proposal.

Most students follow the Proposal Development Track and develop an original thesis proposal. Students graduating in Fall 2026 follow the accelerated Fall Graduation Thesis Completion Track and apply the same workshops to a complete honors thesis. You will learn to identify a research question, navigate University resources, write in the conventions of scientific communication, design or evaluate appropriate research methods, and produce the complete document required by your track.

Students will follow a step-by-step process that starts with choosing and defining a research idea, then proceeds through literature review and the development of a hypothesis, aims, objectives, and a research strategy. The course provides tools for understanding the structure of scientific reports and proposals, literature searches, and basic data interpretation. Students will learn about different research approaches and how to achieve consistency in their research projects. The course guides students in beginning and developing a written research proposal that will satisfy the requirements of their advisors, institution, and funding organizations.

The course is designed for students across all departments in CFANS. Whether your thesis involves field experiments in agronomy, sensory evaluation in food science, econometric models in applied economics, or qualitative interviews in natural resource management, the research process and writing skills taught here apply to your work.

This is a writing-intensive, workshop-style course. You will write every week. Your writing will improve through practice, feedback, and revision, not through a single high-stakes assignment at the end. The final Full Thesis Proposal or Final Thesis Draft is the culmination of a semester of scaffolded writing practice.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  1. identify and articulate a research domain and thesis topic;
  2. navigate library databases and conduct systematic literature searches;
  3. write technical prose using appropriate disciplinary conventions;
  4. distinguish quantitative and qualitative methodologies and select an approach appropriate to a research question;
  5. evaluate statistical claims, study designs, and evidence quality;
  6. construct or revise the major sections of a thesis proposal or thesis;
  7. provide constructive, specific peer feedback;
  8. communicate professionally with mentors, committee members, and other academic professionals; and
  9. complete an honors thesis proposal or thesis draft that meets the applicable track and program requirements.

Course Structure

The course meets twice weekly for discussion, workshop, and writing time. Regular meetings use short periods of focused instruction followed by guided or independent application and a closing discussion. You will do much of the searching, analysis, drafting, and revision in class. Individual meetings replace regular class in Weeks 3, 7, and 13; these are modeled on graduate-student advising. Peer review sessions and working sessions are embedded throughout.

The current week-by-week schedule is maintained on the Schedule page of the course site.

Course Expectations

What I Expect from You

  • Write every week. Writing is how you learn in this course.
  • Complete readings and preparation before class.
  • Participate in peer review with genuine, specific feedback.
  • Communicate proactively with your thesis advisor and with me.
  • Submit assignments on time and ask questions early.
  • Make full use of my office hours.

What You Can Expect from Me

  • Development of materials on the board. No PowerPoint.
  • Clear communication and prompt feedback on your writing.
  • Feedback aimed at your growth as a researcher and writer.
  • Honest assessment of your work. I will not tell you something is good when it is not, and I will not tell you something is bad without telling you how to make it better.
  • Kindness and respect for your lived experience and learning needs. My door is always open.

How This Class Runs

We work mainly at the board and on paper. I do not teach from slide decks. Instead, we build questions, arguments, outlines, and revisions together while the decisions are still visible. Bring paper and something to write with to every session, along with any handout or draft listed for that week.

That choice is deliberate. A finished presentation can hide the choices, false starts, and revisions that produced it. Here, we will make those moves visible: test an idea, notice where it fails, revise it, and explain why the revision is stronger. You will practice the same process in your own proposal work.

The board work is one part of the course record, not the whole of it. The course site provides the schedule, assignment criteria, readings, and materials you need to prepare and follow through. Canvas holds grades, private feedback, protected student records, and the assignments designated for online upload. If you need course materials in another format, contact me early so we can make a workable plan.

Week 4 is the exception. We work on screens that week, because library databases and Zotero are the subject of the lesson and the work requires live access. Bring a laptop for both sessions. You will build and document a search: record the terms you tried, what did not work, what you changed, and why you kept particular sources.

How to Succeed in This Course

Students who succeed in this course typically:

  • attend consistently and come prepared;
  • start writing early and revise often;
  • seek clarification early when concepts are unclear;
  • treat the course as both a technical and a creative endeavor; and
  • contact their thesis advisor early and communicate regularly.

If you find yourself struggling, please reach out during office hours or by appointment, talk to classmates, since peer support is part of the course design, and ask for extensions early. Life happens.

Suggested Materials

No required textbook. Selected readings and discipline-specific proposal examples are listed on the course site, with lawful access through the University Libraries or another named source. These references are useful when you need support in a particular part of the work:

  1. Creswell, John W., and J. David Creswell. 2023. Research Design: Qualitative, Quantitative, and Mixed Methods Approaches, 6th ed. SAGE Publishing. For developing a research question and matching methods to it.
  2. Booth, Wayne C., et al. 2024. The Craft of Research, 5th ed. University of Chicago Press. For moving from a topic to a research problem, using sources well, and building an argument.
  3. Sword, Helen. 2012. Stylish Academic Writing. Harvard University Press. For clearer prose, stronger structure, and revision beyond sentence-level cleanup.
  4. Motulsky, Harvey. 2018. Intuitive Biostatistics: A Nonmathematical Guide to Statistical Thinking, 4th ed. Oxford University Press. For students working with quantitative or experimental research.

Supplementary peer-reviewed articles, thesis proposal exemplars, and University Honors Program documents are listed or linked on the course site. They support work on technical writing conventions, research methods across CFANS disciplines, statistical discernment, and honors college processes.

No statistical software is required in this course. You will need:

  • a stable internet connection, for the course site, Canvas, and library databases;
  • Canvas, for grades, private feedback, protected student records, and the assignments designated for online upload;

Assignment Submission

Every graded milestone appears in the Canvas gradebook, including work handed in on paper. A Canvas gradebook entry does not by itself mean that you should upload a file. The assignment overview and the applicable assignment page state the normal submission method.

  • On paper: Short weekly work, progress reports used as meeting agendas, and student peer-review worksheets are handed in during class or at the end of the individual meeting. Bring a single-sided printed copy unless the assignment page says otherwise.
  • Canvas file upload: Long drafts, document-level revision packages, professional-review evidence, final course documents, and the process portfolio are submitted through Canvas. Follow the file and privacy directions on the assignment page.
  • Instructor recorded: The Week 13 conversation is completed in person. Bring the required conversation brief; the instructor records the result in Canvas, and there is no routine student upload.

The standard submission method for each week is listed on the Assignments Overview. If an absence, disability-related need, or access barrier makes a paper hand-in unworkable, contact the instructor for a digital alternative. An approved alternative changes the delivery method, not the assignment standard or deadline unless we agree to that separately.

Specifications Grading

This course uses specifications grading within the required A-F framework. The grade is determined by which bundle of milestones a student completes at a satisfactory standard, not by percentage weights. There are no points, no curve, and no percentage weights.

“Satisfactory” means the work contains every required part and meets the observable criteria on the relevant assignment page. Time spent or effort claimed does not substitute for those criteria. Instructor feedback on drafts is intended to support development and revision.

There are 15 milestones, one for each week of the term. Each course track has an equivalent 15-milestone sequence on the course schedule and assignments page. When a milestone has several parts, every part must be satisfactory for it to count. The process portfolio is named separately in the bundles above and is not among the 15.

Three milestones are essential for every passing grade: the Week 12 advisor-ready document and release screen, the Week 13 instructor conversation and professional-review submission, and the Week 15 final document. Milestone counts create room for an imperfect semester. They do not create a path around the complete proposal or thesis that the course exists to produce.

How the Week 13 Conversation Is Counted

The Week 13 conversation appears twice in the grading scheme. The two uses measure different things, and it is worth being explicit about which is which.

As milestone 13, it asks whether you did the work. You arrived with the advisor-ready document, you engaged with questions about design and evidence, and you completed the release revisions before sending the document to your Primary Advisor and/or Proposal Committee. Coming prepared and finishing those revisions satisfies milestone 13.

As a rating, it asks how the conversation itself went, judged strong, satisfactory, or needs development against the criteria on the Week 13 page. The rating is what separates the A and B bundles.

A rating of “needs development” does not by itself cost you milestone 13. Like any milestone rated not yet satisfactory, the rating can be re-earned: schedule a follow-up conversation within one week.

What Counts as a Complete Portfolio

The process portfolio has four phase sections, a mentor interaction log, and a final reflection. The bundles above use these three standards.

The course assigns straight A, B, C, D, or F grades. Plus and minus modifiers are not used.

The scaffolded assignment structure, regular progress reports, and three rounds of individual meetings exist to get you there. To support that, I offer Study Hall Hours, by appointment, where we review current assignments, work through writing and research questions, and take on common problems together.

How Much Feedback Each Submission Gets

Not everything you submit carries the same weight of instructor response.

Progress Reports

Progress reports are the backbone of the early course. Proposal Development students submit Progress Reports 1, 2, and 3. Fall Graduation students submit two thesis progress reports. Reports connected to Weeks 3 and 7 serve as individual-meeting agendas. This is the same structure I use with my graduate students. You do not show up to a meeting empty-handed.

Each progress report uses a structured template:

  • What did you accomplish since the last report? Be specific: dates, meetings, hours.
  • What did you struggle with? How did you try to resolve it?
  • What is your plan for the next period? Name specific tasks and deadlines.
  • What questions do you have for me?

Progress means different things at different points in the semester. Early on it might mean identifying your domain and contacting potential mentors. Late in the semester it means drafting and revising thesis sections.

The Progress Reports page carries the full template, what counts as satisfactory at each stage, and the Professional Review Plan.

Writing Assignments

Writing assignments build progressively. Early ones are low-stakes exercises; later ones are thesis section drafts. Each includes specific criteria tied to that module’s objectives. Written feedback is provided on all of them.

Peer Reviews

There are two structured student peer review sessions, in Weeks 9 and 11. You review classmates’ writing using a structured protocol with specific criteria for each thesis section. Peer review is a core component of this course: structured feedback from a different reader catches what you cannot see in your own draft.

Professional Review

Week 14 is a professional review, not a third student peer review. Students send the released complete document to the Primary Advisor and/or Proposal Committee by Nov 25, after the Week 12 release screen and before the Week 13 instructor conversation. The reviewers should already have reserved the review window and should plan to return feedback around Dec 9. Students submit review evidence, a response memo, and a prioritized revision plan. The grade reflects preparation, communication, and use of feedback, not another person’s response speed.

Full Thesis Proposal (Course Assignment)

The Full Thesis Proposal is the substantial course document and the culmination of the semester’s work. It includes an abstract, introduction, literature review, methods, expected results, discussion/significance, conclusion, and references. It must meet both your department’s and the University Honors Program’s requirements.

Do not confuse this document with the UHP Thesis Proposal Form. The UHP form is a shorter administrative submission filed through the Honors Reporting Center. It asks for your thesis title, a brief prospectus, and your committee chair. It is not a substitute for the Full Thesis Proposal.

The Week 13 instructor conversation is about your developing Full Thesis Proposal or thesis; it is not the UHP filing deadline. For spring or summer graduates, the UHP Thesis Proposal Form is due December 1. Confirm the current date and any earlier department deadline with your advisor and departmental Honors Faculty Representative.

Final Thesis Draft (Fall Graduation Track)

Students graduating in Fall 2026 submit a complete thesis draft rather than a new proposal. They should already have filed the UHP Thesis Proposal Form, formed the Proposal Committee, and completed enough research to support thesis completion. Their final thesis and UHP Thesis Completion Form are due December 14, 2026. The Dec 16 course submission consists of the process portfolio and a record of the completion submission. Committee approval is due December 21 and remains an institutional decision rather than a course grade.

Resubmission

Any milestone rated “not yet satisfactory” may be revised and resubmitted once within one week. I will give you specific feedback on what needs to change.

Extra Credit

This course does not offer extra credit. Instead it is built with multiple pathways for improvement: low-stakes early writing that builds toward higher-stakes drafts, structured peer review that arrives before major submissions, progress reports that let you course-correct, and a resubmission policy on any milestone rated not yet satisfactory. The grading structure rewards consistency and growth rather than single high-stakes moments. You are never out of options.

Expected Student Workload

This is a 3-credit course. University policy sets one credit at approximately 45 hours of academic work across the semester. Three credits is therefore about 135 hours, or roughly 9 hours per week across the 15-week term.

Two things worth saying plainly about that number.

The 2.5 hours of class time is fixed, but it does not hold steady across the term. In Weeks 3, 7, and 13 regular class is replaced by your individual meeting, which runs 15 minutes in Weeks 3 and 7 and 20 minutes in Week 13. At the enrollment cap, those meetings require 300 minutes in Weeks 3 and 7 and 400 minutes in Week 13. The two scheduled class periods provide 150 minutes. Before the first class, the week-specific Google Booking pages will therefore publish the full required capacity: all in-class slots plus at least 150 minutes of additional appointment blocks in Weeks 3 and 7 and at least 250 minutes in Week 13. If none of those blocks is accessible with your class, work, care, or transportation schedule, contact me for a structured asynchronous document review and follow-up rather than losing the meeting. Those weeks still buy you back several hours. Spend them on the draft.

The 6.5 hours outside class is an average, not a schedule. It will not distribute evenly. Weeks with a substantive draft due, Weeks 8 and 10 in particular, will run well above it. Weeks after a peer review will run below. Plan for the peaks rather than the average, because a proposal or thesis is not the kind of work you can compress into the last available evening.

See the University policy on expected work per course credit.

Attendance and Engagement

Attendance is expected, because this course runs on discussion, peer review, writing workshops, and individual meetings. Attendance is not graded directly. Engagement is documented instead through observable evidence: participation in peer review sessions, submission of progress reports and writing assignments, and attendance at the individual meetings in Weeks 3, 7, and 13.

Students who miss class regularly will find it difficult to participate in peer review or to benefit from the workshops. If you are sick or unable to attend, communicate with me. Your wellbeing comes first. University rules for excused absences and makeup work apply.

Late Work

This is a scaffolded writing course where assignments build on each other. Late submissions delay your own progress and can affect peer review scheduling.

  • Progress reports and writing assignments: communicate with me before the deadline if you need an extension. Extensions are generally granted when requested in advance.
  • Peer reviews: cannot be late. Your classmates depend on receiving your feedback on time.
  • Final proposal: the Dec 16 deadline is firm.
  • Fall Graduation final thesis: the Dec 14 UHP completion deadline is firm. A late thesis may jeopardize graduation with Latin Honors.
  • A first late submission carries no penalty when communicated in advance. Repeated late submissions without communication are addressed individually.

Revision and Feedback

Drafting and revision are core course activities. Retain peer, instructor, and mentor feedback, and document how major feedback affected the proposal. Rough drafts are expected. Feedback is developmental. Revision is the work.

Honors Thesis Requirements

University Honors Program Requirements

To graduate with Latin Honors you must complete an honors thesis in your major department. Your thesis must:

  • go beyond typical coursework, and cannot be a repurposed project from another course;
  • demonstrate your ability to synthesize existing work and present it succinctly;
  • show capacity to propose and execute a research idea within a timeline; and
  • produce writing at a publishable or public-presentation level of quality.

The full requirements document is posted on this site: Honors Requirements, University Honors Program (PDF). Read the thesis sections. Everything below summarizes that document, and where the two disagree, the Honors Program document governs.

Committee Composition

Your thesis committee has at least three members: a primary advisor and two readers. Two rules constrain who those people can be, and students routinely learn about the second one too late.

  • At least one member, though not necessarily your primary advisor, must be tenured or tenure-track faculty at the University of Minnesota.
  • At least one member must be affiliated with the department in which the thesis is conducted.

Beyond those two constraints, the remaining members may be tenured or tenure-track University faculty, or a non-tenure-track University instructor or researcher with specialized knowledge relevant to your topic. Members do not all have to come from the same program or college, and a committee that spans units is sometimes an advantage rather than a complication.

Anyone further afield, a community member, faculty at another institution, or an advanced graduate student, can serve only in rare cases, and only when your department petitions the Honors Program Director for approval. Do not assemble a committee assuming that route is open to you. If you need it, ask early.

Composition rules vary by department and may change. Confirm your committee structure with your department’s honors representative and against the current University Honors Program requirements before finalizing your committee.

Key Deadlines

Both forms are filed through the Honors Reporting Center. The proposal form asks for your thesis title, a brief prospectus, and your committee chair; the remaining members are listed on the final form. The completion form must include a PDF of the thesis, and it prompts your committee members to confirm their approval and the level of honors. Neither UHP form replaces the Full Thesis Proposal assignment in this course.

These dates are specific to Fall 2026. Look up the current University Honors Program dates when planning a different graduation term.

These reflect Honors Program timelines and are subject to change. Confirm current dates on the University Honors Program site and with your department’s honors faculty representative. Department-specific deadlines take precedence. If you and your advisor agree to a different timeline, put it in writing.

Use of Generative AI

We do not use AI tools in this course. No assignment requires one, no assignment expects one, and you do not need one to do well here.

That is not a technophobic position. It is about what this particular course is for. You are learning to take a vague interest and turn it into a question someone could actually answer, then defend that reasoning in writing. The value is in the struggle: the stalled paragraph, the hypothesis that collapses when you try to state what varies against what, the draft you rewrite four times. A paragraph that arrives already smooth tells me nothing about where your thinking is stuck, which means I cannot help you. And you will be asked to defend this proposal to an advisor and a committee who will assume every sentence is yours.

What you submit must be your own thinking and your own words.

I may demonstrate an AI tool in class if it is useful to a point I am making. That is my call as the instructor, and a demonstration does not open the door to using one on your own work.

Honest representation of your sources, data, collaboration, writing, and tool use is required under the Student Conduct Code. If you are ever unsure whether something crosses a line, ask me before you submit, not after.

Students seeking formal accommodations or resources should contact the Disability Resource Center at drc@umn.edu. If you have a current accommodation letter, please share it with me as soon as possible so we can discuss how your accommodations will be implemented in this course.

Scholastic Dishonesty

Academic integrity standards are detailed in Teaching and Learning: Instructor and Unit Responsibilities. As students in a university community, you are expected to do your own academic work and to cite the sources of knowledge you draw on. Failing to do so is scholastic dishonesty, which includes plagiarism; cheating on assignments or examinations, including unauthorized use of online learning support and testing platforms; unauthorized collaboration, including posting student-generated coursework on platforms not approved for the course; taking, acquiring, or using course materials without faculty permission; submitting false or incomplete records of academic achievement; acting alone or with another to falsify records or dishonestly obtain grades, honors, awards, or professional endorsement; altering, forging, misrepresenting, or misusing a University academic record; or fabricating or falsifying data, research procedures, or data analysis.

When it is determined that a student has cheated, the student may be given an F for the course and may face additional sanctions from the University. The Office for Community Standards and the Center for Educational Innovation offer additional resources on academic integrity. Be wary of sites that advertise themselves as tutoring websites: it is not permissible to upload instructor materials to these sites without permission, or to copy material for your assignments from them. When you have questions, speak with or email me to clarify the practices expected in this course.

Excused Absences and Makeup Work

Such circumstances include verified illness, participation in intercollegiate athletic events, subpoenas, jury duty, military service, bereavement, and religious observances. Appropriate Use of Notes and Classroom Materials

Sexual Harassment, Sexual Assault, Stalking, and Relationship Violence

Policies are covered in the Sexual Misconduct Policy. The University prohibits sexual misconduct and encourages anyone experiencing it to access resources for personal support and reporting. If you want to speak confidentially with someone about an experience of sexual misconduct, contact a confidential resource on campus. If you wish to report sexual misconduct or have questions about the University’s policies and procedures, contact the campus Title IX office.

Instructors are required to share information they learn about possible sexual misconduct with the campus Title IX office. This allows a Title IX staff member to reach out to those who have experienced sexual misconduct to provide information about personal support resources and options for investigation. You may talk to me about concerns related to sexual misconduct, and I will provide support and keep what you share private to the extent possible given my University role.

Academic Freedom and Responsibility

Academic freedom is a cornerstone of the University. Within the scope and content of the course as defined by the instructor, it includes the freedom to discuss relevant matters in the classroom and to conduct appropriate research. With that freedom comes responsibility. Students are encouraged to develop critical judgment and to engage in a sustained and independent search for truth. Students are free to take reasoned exception to the views offered in any course of study and to reserve judgment about matters of opinion, but they are responsible for learning the content of any course in which they are enrolled.

When conducting research, relevant institutional approvals must be obtained and the research must be consistent with University policy.

Reports of concerns about academic freedom are taken seriously.
