Thesis Proposal: Guiding Questions

Developing Your Research Plan

How to Use This Guide

Your proposal should help a reader understand what you want to investigate, why the question matters, and how you will answer it. Use the questions below to develop your ideas before turning them into connected paragraphs. Follow your department’s required format; some of these sections may be combined.

Keep your research question and claims-and-evidence ledger beside you as you draft. The ledger records a claim, its supporting source and location, and the limits on what that source supports. Revisit the Week 1 ledger guidance if you need a reminder. Your question guides the research plan; the ledger helps you check its evidence.

Use the printable Thesis Proposal Planning Worksheet to record working answers, supporting passages, and unresolved decisions. The worksheet has four pages and groups related questions so you can work on the parts your draft needs. Short notes are enough at this stage.

These are planning questions, not 15 required headings or a new submission checklist. Follow your department’s format and the assignment for the draft you are preparing. For the Week 10 draft, focus on the introduction, literature review, and methods; use the other questions to plan the full proposal as it develops. The assignment page lists the required accompanying materials.

For Week 9 Monday’s mapping activity, use the questions relevant to your problem, rationale, method, and contribution. You do not need to answer the entire guide in class. Mark what you do not yet know and name how you will find out.

Before You Begin: What Have You Already Built?

Return to the question framework you developed in Week 5. If a comparison fits your project, revisit PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome):

If your question is descriptive, exploratory, or qualitative, use the question structure that fits your project. Identify the people or system, the phenomenon or process, the context, and what you want to understand. Do not invent a treatment or comparison to fill an empty slot.

Next, review your claims ledger. Which claims have adequate support? Which need narrower wording? Where do sources disagree? Which questions remain unresolved?

1. Title page: What is this project about?

Your working title should identify the subject and focus of the proposed research.

  • What population, organism, system, or problem am I studying?
  • What relationship, comparison, or process am I investigating?
  • Does the setting belong in the title because it defines the project’s scope?
  • Does my title describe the study without assuming what I will find?
  • Which identifying details does my department require, such as my name, advisor, program, and date?

Use the most useful elements of your research question to make the title specific. You do not need to fit every element into it. Revisit the title after drafting the proposal.

2. Abstract or project summary: Can I explain the whole plan briefly?

Write this section after the main proposal so it reflects the project you actually designed.

  • What problem motivates the study?
  • What specific gap or unresolved issue will I address?
  • What is my main research question or objective?
  • What approach will I use?
  • What do I expect the project to produce or clarify?
  • Why would that contribution matter?

A reader should be able to identify the question, approach, and value of the project from this summary. Describe anticipated findings as expectations, and identify completed preliminary work as completed.

3. Introduction and background: What does my reader need to understand first?

Move from the broader topic toward the particular problem your thesis addresses.

  • What situation, process, or issue introduces my topic?
  • Which terms or concepts does my reader need defined?
  • What is already established about this topic?
  • What population, system, or setting will I focus on?
  • How does this background lead to my specific research problem?

Use your claims ledger to select the background claims that the reader needs. For each one, ask: Does the source support this wording, in this population and context? Keep the qualifications that matter.

4. Problem statement and significance: What needs to be understood, and why does it matter?

Explain the unresolved issue so a reader can see why your study is worth doing.

  • What remains uncertain, inconsistent, poorly explained, or insufficiently examined?
  • Is this a practical problem, a gap in knowledge, or both?
  • Who needs a better answer, and what decision or understanding could it inform?
  • What evidence shows that this problem exists?
  • What part of the problem can my thesis reasonably address?

Check your gap statement against your ledger and search record. “The studies I reviewed did not examine this population” is a bounded claim. “No one has studied this” requires much stronger support.

5. Literature review: What does the existing evidence allow me to say?

Organize this section around ideas, findings, methods, and disagreements that matter to your question.

  • What are the main themes in the literature?
  • Where do studies reach similar conclusions?
  • Where do findings differ?
  • Could differences in populations, treatments, comparisons, measurements, or settings help explain that disagreement?
  • Which study limitations affect what I can conclude?
  • How does this body of work lead to my proposed study?

Use your claims ledger to group related claims and compare the sources supporting them. If you also have a synthesis matrix, use it to compare study characteristics across papers.

For each paragraph, ask: What claim am I making? Which sources support or complicate it? What qualification does the evidence require? How does it move the reader toward my question?

6. Research questions, objectives, and hypotheses: What exactly will I investigate?

State the question your methods must answer.

  • What is my primary research question?
  • Does it identify the relevant population or system, intervention or exposure, comparison, and outcome where appropriate?
  • Are any secondary questions necessary and feasible?
  • What concrete objectives will help me answer the main question?
  • If I propose a hypothesis, what evidence or reasoning supports that prediction?
  • What finding would challenge my hypothesis?

Keep these distinctions clear: a question identifies what you want to know; an objective states what you will accomplish; a hypothesis predicts a relationship or difference.

Check that your question follows from the gap established in your literature review. Exploratory work may need clear questions and objectives without a directional hypothesis.

7. Theoretical or conceptual framework: What ideas explain the relationships I am investigating?

Include this as a separate section when it serves your project or your department requires it.

  • What theory, model, or set of concepts guides my thinking?
  • How might the proposed relationship or process work?
  • Which concepts need definitions?
  • What assumptions am I making?
  • Are there competing explanations?
  • How will this framework influence what I measure, observe, or ask?

A diagram may help you show proposed relationships. Distinguish relationships supported by existing evidence from relationships your study will examine. Use the ledger to check those distinctions.

8. Methods or proposed approach: How will I produce evidence that answers my question?

Describe the work in enough detail for a reader to judge whether the plan is appropriate and feasible.

  • What research design fits my question, and why?
  • Who or what will I study? Where and when?
  • How will I select participants, plots, samples, documents, datasets, or other sources?
  • What sample size or amount of material is feasible, and how will I justify it?
  • What procedures, instruments, or protocols will I use?
  • How will I define and measure each outcome or examine each concept?
  • How will I analyze the information?
  • What sources of bias, confounding, or measurement error need attention?
  • What permissions, ethical review, access agreements, or training must be in place?

Trace each part of your question into the methods. If your question asks about a comparison, where does that comparison occur in the design? If it names an outcome, how and when will you assess it?

Use your ledger to locate methodological precedents. Explain why a published approach fits your project and what adaptations you need.

9. Expected outcomes: What do I reasonably anticipate finding or producing?

Explain your expectations and their basis while keeping room for evidence that surprises you.

  • What pattern, relationship, difference, or understanding might emerge?
  • Which findings in my claims ledger support that expectation?
  • How closely do those studies match my population, setting, and methods?
  • What other outcomes are plausible?
  • What could I learn if the findings differ from my prediction or remain inconclusive?
  • What tangible products will the project produce, such as a dataset, thematic analysis, map, model, or thesis?

Distinguish three things:

  • The outcome you will assess: the variable, experience, or process named in your question.
  • The result you anticipate: what you tentatively expect to observe.
  • The product you will deliver: what the completed project will produce.

For exploratory or qualitative work, describe the understanding you hope to develop without deciding the themes in advance. Label any hypothetical figures as hypothetical.

10. Expected contribution and possible implications: What could these outcomes help us understand?

Connect the possible findings back to the problem that motivated your study.

  • What could my study clarify, extend, test, or challenge?
  • How would it address the gap identified in my literature review?
  • Who could use this knowledge, and for what purpose?
  • How would different possible findings change the interpretation?
  • What recommendations would require additional evidence?
  • What would remain unanswered even if the project succeeds?

Use your ledger to compare your anticipated contribution with what existing studies already establish. Keep the contribution proportional to the design and scope. Depending on your required format, this material may appear in a proposed discussion section.

11. Scope and limitations: Where will my conclusions stop?

Explain the boundaries you chose and the constraints that may affect interpretation.

  • What populations, locations, conditions, or periods will I include?
  • What will I deliberately leave outside the project?
  • Which constraints involve time, resources, access, sample size, or available data?
  • What uncertainty will remain after analysis?
  • Which limitations can I reduce through design?
  • To whom or what might the findings reasonably apply?

Compare these boundaries with the limits recorded in your claims ledger. If previous studies had a limitation that your project also has, acknowledge it.

12. Timeline, resources, and feasibility: Can I complete this plan?

Build the schedule around the work required to answer your question.

  • What are the major stages: approvals, preparation, recruitment or sampling, data collection, analysis, writing, and revision?
  • Which tasks depend on earlier tasks being completed?
  • Are there seasonal windows, access restrictions, or other fixed dates?
  • What equipment, software, funding, training, and advisor support will I need?
  • When will I request feedback?
  • Where have I allowed time for delays and revision?
  • What will I change if recruitment, access, or data collection falls short?

Give each major task a target date and a concrete sign of completion. If a budget is required, connect each cost to a necessary part of the methods.

13. References: Can a reader trace my evidence?

Provide complete references for the sources cited in the proposal.

  • Does every in-text citation have a matching reference entry?
  • Does every reference entry correspond to a source cited in the text?
  • Have I checked author names, dates, titles, and publication details?
  • Have I read and verified the sources supporting my central claims?
  • Does each citation support the sentence where I placed it?
  • Is the formatting consistent with the required citation style?

Your claims ledger helps you track evidence, but readers still need normal scholarly citations. Check the original source when wording, context, or support is uncertain.

14. Appendices: What supporting material helps someone evaluate the plan?

Include materials that support the proposal but would interrupt its main argument.

  • Would readers benefit from seeing my interview guide, survey, protocol, or data collection form?
  • Do they need a detailed analysis plan, search strategy, or supplementary table?
  • Would a conceptual diagram or preliminary chapter outline help?
  • Has my instructor or advisor requested the claims ledger as a supporting document?
  • Have I labeled each appendix and referred to it in the main text?

Keep essential explanations in the proposal itself. A reader should understand your design without having to reconstruct it from the appendices.

15. Brief conclusion, if required: Does the proposal hold together?

Close by reconnecting the question, approach, and contribution.

  • What specific problem will this study address?
  • Why is the proposed approach suited to that problem?
  • What could the completed work help us understand?
  • Have I kept the population, comparison, and outcomes consistent throughout?
  • Have I introduced any promises that the methods cannot fulfill?

Before submitting, trace one complete path through your draft: evidence in the ledger → unresolved question → research objective → method → possible outcome → justified contribution. Wherever that connection breaks, revise the proposal.
