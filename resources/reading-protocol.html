Reading Protocol

How to Take a Research Article Apart

Use this as a starting procedure when the course puts an empirical research paper in front of you. Keep it with your drafts and mark up the article in a format you can use. The procedure is a heuristic, not a universal map. Some disciplines use recognizable introduction, methods, results, and discussion sections. Others organize questions, arguments, observations, themes, cases, or models differently.

Do Not Read It Front to Back

The first instinct may be to start at the first word and read to the last one. For many empirical papers, a targeted first pass makes the structure visible before you read closely.

Read in this order instead.

The time required depends on the paper, the discipline, and your purpose. A targeted pass helps you decide where closer reading is needed.

The Four Questions

For any paper, on any pass, you are answering these. Write the answers in the margins.

  1. What question were the authors trying to answer? State it as a question, in your own words, in one sentence.
  2. What did they conclude? Find the sentence in the paper that says it. Underline it. Do not paraphrase yet.
  3. What evidence is that conclusion resting on? Point at the specific result, observation, estimate, figure, table, quotation, theme, case, model, passage, or other form of evidence. If you cannot find one, that is a finding.
  4. Where are the seams? Mark where each section starts and name the job it is doing.

Question 3 is where the most useful work begins. If the connection is not immediately visible, you have found something worth investigating, not made a mistake. Question 2 gives you the authors’ conclusion. Question 3 asks what is holding that conclusion up. Learning to trace the connection between the two is a skill, and each careful attempt strengthens it.

What Each Section Is Doing

Many empirical research articles and proposals contain the parts below, even when the labels, order, or boundaries differ. Once you can see the work each part is doing, you can compare that structure with other disciplinary forms and with your own draft.

Two of these are worth flagging now. Results and discussion are different jobs, even when a discipline combines them in one section. Watch how the authors move from reporting evidence to interpreting it. You will need to make that move visible in your own work later in the semester.

The Second Pass

Everything above is structural. It tells you what a paper says. It does not tell you whether to believe it.

The evaluative pass comes in Week 6, where you take each claim and ask how many were studied, how large the effect was, and what the figures are not showing. Do not try to run both passes at once early in the semester. Find the parts first. Judge them second.

Common Traps

  • Looking up every unfamiliar term. On a first pass, keep going. Circle the terms and come back. Stopping at each one means you never see the shape.
  • Trusting the abstract. The abstract is the most confident and least qualified text in the paper. Check it against the results.
  • Reading the methods first and getting stuck. Methods sections are dense by design. They are step 5 for a reason.
  • Taking notes instead of marking up. Notes drift into summary. Marking up keeps you anchored to what the paper actually says.
  • Treating a paper as settled. A published paper is one contribution to an argument that is still running, not the final word on it.

When You Read for Your Own Project

Add one question to the four above: what does this change about my question? Record the answer in your source notes while it is fresh. That running record is what makes the synthesis matrix in Week 8 possible, and it is part of your process portfolio.
