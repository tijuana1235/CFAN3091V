Expected and Actual Results: A Worked Example

Week 11 | Claims, Evidence, and Interpretation

ImportantMade-up study and data

This entire study is fictional. Every numerical observation below is made up for teaching. No field experiment was conducted, and these values are not research evidence about winter rye or soybean. The passages labeled “actual-results style” demonstrate how to report observations; their numbers are still invented. Do not cite this example as a research finding or use these values as data in your own proposal or thesis.

Before collecting data, what can you responsibly say about the results? Use this example with Week 11: Expected Results and Discussion and the Two-Outcome Table worksheet.

In expected results, describe the evidence that would support a proposed claim and what alternative outcomes would mean. In actual results, report the evidence you obtained and build or revise the claim from that evidence. Existing literature helps justify a prediction; the proposed study will produce data that test it.

1. Start with the Question and Measurements

Do winter rye plots have lower weed density than no-cover plots during the first 28 days after soybean planting?

For this fictional example, assume this study design:

  • One field site, one growing season.
  • Four blocks, each containing two plots. Randomly assign rye and no-cover treatments within each block, giving four plots per treatment.
  • Leave terminated rye residue on the soil in the rye treatment; keep other crop management the same between treatments.
  • Count weeds in a marked 1 m² quadrat in each plot at 14 and 28 days after planting. Count without removing the weeds, using the same quadrat at both dates.
  • Record weed density as plants/m² and retain the block, plot, treatment, and sampling date with each observation. Do not measure soybean yield.

This is a simplified teaching design, not an approved field protocol.

Before reading the table, write one claim these measurements could help you evaluate. What comparison would you need?

The yield claim exposes a missing measurement. Weed counts alone cannot answer the yield question.

2. Construct an Expected Result

Adopt this working hypothesis for the exercise:

Winter rye reduces early weed establishment, producing lower weed density through day 28.

This is an assumption being tested in the fictional example. In your own proposal, explain the basis for your expectation using relevant literature, preliminary observations, or a stated theoretical rationale. This example supplies no verified literature finding about rye.

Make the prediction specific:

  • Lower than what? The no-cover treatment.
  • When? At 14 and 28 days after planting.
  • Measured how? Weed plants per square meter.
  • What would require reconsideration? Similar or higher densities, or a reduction that disappears by day 28.

Model Expected-Results Paragraph

We expect weed density to be lower in winter rye plots than in no-cover plots at 14 and 28 days after soybean planting. Lower densities at both dates would support the prediction that the reduction persists through the measured period. A difference at day 14 followed by similar densities at day 28 would instead suggest a temporary reduction. We will evaluate the treatment differences alongside variation among plots and uncertainty in the estimates.

The paragraph specifies a measurable pattern without inventing numerical findings. Exact predicted values would need a defensible basis. A sketch can show the anticipated direction without pretending to contain observations.

3. Examine the Made-up Observations

Imagine the study has been completed. Keep the earlier prediction visible, then write two sentences describing what the table shows: one for day 14 and one for day 28.

All values in this table are made up. They are not collected field data.

Model Actual-Results Style, Using Made-up Data

In this fictional dataset, at 14 days after planting, mean weed density was 25 plants/m² in rye plots and 50 plants/m² in no-cover plots, a difference of 25 plants/m². Rye plots had lower density in all four blocks. At 28 days, both treatments averaged 70 plants/m²; rye density was higher in two blocks and lower in two.

These are descriptive results. A completed analysis would also report uncertainty and account for the paired blocks and repeated measurements of the same plots. Equal sample means alone do not establish that the treatments have no effect. No significance test is supplied in this example.

Notice the change in language: “we expect” and “would support” describe a prediction and its possible interpretation. “Mean density was” reports observations. Changing the verb tense alone is not enough; a real results section needs real evidence.

4. Revise the Claims and Interpret the Pattern

Model Discussion, Using the Fictional Outcome

In this fictional dataset, the observed pattern is consistent with an early reduction in weed density, but the difference in treatment means did not persist to day 28. This limits the proposed claim of suppression throughout the first month. The study does not establish why the pattern changed or whether it affected soybean yield. Those questions would require additional measurements, and evidence from this site and season would not establish performance across other growing conditions.

Could we say that rye blocked light and therefore reduced weeds? That is a possible explanation to investigate. This study did not measure light or isolate that mechanism.

In your own discussion, connect your interpretation to the actual sources in your literature review. Explain whether the pattern agrees with, differs from, or leaves questions about that work. No literature connection is invented for this teaching example.

5. Apply the Sequence to Your Own Work

Choose one proposed claim and write:

  1. The measurement and comparison that would test it.
  2. The pattern you expect and the basis for that expectation.
  3. A plausible alternative pattern.
  4. What each pattern would allow you to conclude.
  5. A claim that would remain beyond the evidence your methods collect.

Trade with a partner. Identify any claim that requires a measurement the proposed methods will not collect. Use that finding to revise the claim or identify a design question to discuss with your advisor.

For the Fall Graduation track, use your actual observations to check the claims in your Results and Discussion. Keep predictions distinguishable from findings, and use only your real data for the results.

The first prediction in this example included a reduction lasting through day 28. The invented observations required a narrower claim. The expected-results paragraph prepared us to interpret that outcome; the actual-results style paragraph described it.
