Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish informal explanation from technical academic prose;
  • revise sentences for precision, clarity, and appropriate qualification;
  • narrow a research domain to a claim that could turn out to be wrong;
  • write a first-contact message a specific faculty member can act on;
  • identify the required parts of an honors thesis proposal;
  • locate the requirements that apply to your program; and
  • connect those requirements to a working proposal timeline.

Monday: Technical Writing Fundamentals

Technical writing helps another reader inspect your reasoning. The goal is not to sound complicated. The goal is to make the relationship among the question, evidence, method, and conclusion visible.

Topic Funnel

Your Interest, Motivation, and Direction Freewrite, shortened here to the Freewrite, may contain several interests, a possible direction, or only the beginning of a question. Progress Report 1 asks you to name a domain and say a rough research question out loud. This drill covers that distance in four steps.

  1. Broad domain. Write the area you care about in one phrase.
  2. Focused problem. Name one thing inside it that is unknown, contested, or going wrong.
  3. Variable comparison. Name what you would compare against what.
  4. Testable claim. Write one sentence that could turn out to be wrong.

The Davis paper from Week 1 gives us a complete worked example:

Then choose one of these practice domains with a partner: calf health, food storage, pollinator habitat, household adoption of energy upgrades, music practice, or historical newspaper coverage. These are starting points, not facts. Your job is to supply a focused problem, a comparison, and a claim that could be checked.

For an experimental or observational question, try this sentence frame:

In [population or system], [outcome] will [increase, decrease, or differ] under [condition or exposure] compared with [reference condition].

Not every thesis is an experiment. For a qualitative, historical, or interpretive project, the comparison may involve cases, time periods, texts, groups, or competing explanations:

Across [cases or sources], [pattern or interpretation] will be more evident under [condition, period, or context] than under [comparison].

Expect the first few attempts to stall at step three. That is the hard step. A topic becomes investigable when you can name the difference, relationship, pattern, or interpretation you will examine. The funnel is a starting structure, not a demand that every field use the same kind of hypothesis.

Informal Notes and Technical Prose

Informal writing records what you are noticing. Technical prose lets another reader inspect exactly what you mean and how far the evidence reaches. The difference is not bigger words.

Use three questions when you revise: What exactly happened? Compared with what? Under which conditions?

Diagnostic Revision

Use one paragraph from the Freewrite. That assignment earns a Satisfactory or Not Yet Satisfactory participation result against its exploratory standard. Grammar, certainty, and polish are not part of that standard. Here, you are using a paragraph you already wrote to see what changes when a reader needs to inspect the reasoning.

  1. Select one paragraph from the Freewrite.
  2. Mark its main claim, evidence, and reasoning. If one is absent, mark the place where a reader would need it.
  3. Identify vague terms, unsupported certainty, and unnecessary wording.
  4. Revise the paragraph without changing its meaning.
  5. Explain which revision most improved the paragraph and why.

Complete the topic funnel and diagnostic revision on the Week 2 Writing Workshop worksheet. You will submit both as Milestone 2a. Submit the original Freewrite separately as Milestone 1 at the same time.

Between Monday and Wednesday

Wednesday runs at the board and on paper. Complete the screen-based lookup before class and bring printed pages or handwritten notes.

  1. Read the thesis sections of the Honors Requirements, University Honors Program (PDF).
  2. Find your major on the University Honors Program’s Major and Thesis Requirements directory, then locate any proposal guidance published by your program. Record the source, required sections, approvals, deadlines, and anything you cannot confirm.
  3. Identify three possible faculty advisors. For each person, record their name and unit, one specific paper, project, or course, and one sentence connecting that work to the focused problem from your topic funnel. Use Experts@Minnesota and departmental pages as starting points.

If published program guidance is unavailable, write down where you looked and the person or office you think can resolve the question. That is useful audit work, not a blank response.

Wednesday: Thesis Requirements

Proposal requirements vary across programs. You are responsible for finding the requirements that apply to your degree and confirming them with the appropriate program contact.

Start from the Honors Program’s own document: Honors Requirements, University Honors Program (PDF). The thesis sections cover committee composition, the three thesis plans, and the filing deadlines. Read them before class. Two rules catch people out: at least one committee member must be tenured or tenure-track at the University, and at least one must be affiliated with the department where the thesis is conducted. Those can be the same person, but often are not.

Requirements Audit

Keep two deliverables separate in your notes:

  • Full Thesis Proposal: the substantial document developed in this course.
  • UHP Thesis Proposal Form: the shorter administrative form containing the title, brief prospectus, and committee chair.

The UHP form is not a substitute for the Full Thesis Proposal. The University Honors Program sets a baseline, while your department may require additional sections, approvals, or an oral presentation. Confirm both sources before you set your timeline.

  • Start with your major in the UHP Major and Thesis Requirements directory.
  • Check the official program or department page linked there.
  • Record required sections, approvals, and deadlines.
  • Mark any requirement that is unclear or unavailable.
  • For an open program or curriculum question, use the official CFANS Directors of Undergraduate Studies (DUGS) directory. For an honors-specific question, contact the Honors Faculty Representative listed on your major’s UHP page or your Honors Advisor. The CFANS academic advising directory provides the current advising route.
  • Add the confirmed requirements to your working timeline.
ImportantDo Not Borrow Another Program’s Requirements

Do not infer a requirement from another department’s materials. If your own program does not publish the answer, mark it unconfirmed, record where you looked, and ask the appropriate program or honors contact. An honest open question is better than a false requirement.

Outreach Protocol

A generic message asking “can I do research with you?” gives the recipient little information for deciding how to respond. A useful first-contact message names a specific connection and makes a concrete request. Three paragraphs, under 200 words:

  1. Who you are. Name, major, year, and that you are developing an honors thesis proposal in CFAN 3091V this semester.
  2. Why them. Name the specific paper, project, or course you found before class, and explain its connection to the focused problem you funneled on Monday.
  3. What you are asking for. A 20-minute conversation, with two or three times you are available.

The Advisor FAQ explains the course and includes a complete example message. Use its structure, but replace every bracketed field and generic phrase with details specific to you and the recipient.

Draft the strongest of the three messages in class. Then trade with a partner and test one thing: could this message have been sent to any faculty member in the college? If yes, paragraph two is not doing its work. Revise that message, then use it as a structural model when you complete the other two before your deadline on Friday, September 18.

Before you leave Wednesday, hand in the completed Requirements and Outreach worksheet. I will read it and return brief feedback. This is an in-class participation check based on the work you submit, not on attendance alone. Satisfactory work records the requirements search, analyzes three candidates, includes one complete message draft, and documents the peer test.

Complete the other two message drafts by Friday, September 18. Send all three messages no later than Monday, September 21. Keep dated copies for the Week 4 milestone fallback.

Bring the sent messages and any replies to your Week 3 meeting. We can revise the next action there, but the meeting is not the release point. This schedule leaves the five-business-day follow-up window intact before the Week 4 milestone.

Apply the writing diagnostic and topic funnel to the central argument of your current thesis.
