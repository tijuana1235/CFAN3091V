Week 5: Research Methods and Your Research Question

Phase 2: Evidence and Methods

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research paradigms;
  • identify which paradigm your research question requires, and say why;
  • refine a research question using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework or a disciplinary equivalent; and
  • state the significance of your question against the “so what?” test.

Monday: Research Paradigms: Quantitative, Qualitative, and Mixed Methods

Format: Focused instruction + question-classification workshop

Topics

  • Three ways of knowing: quantitative (how much? how many?), qualitative (how? why? what is the experience?), mixed methods (both, strategically)
  • When to use each: matching your question to your paradigm
  • Examples from CFAN disciplines:
    • Quantitative: yield trials, nutrient analysis, survey data
    • Qualitative: farmer decision-making, community food systems, lived experience
    • Mixed: adoption of cover crops (survey data + farmer interviews)
  • Common misconceptions: “qualitative isn’t rigorous” (it is), “quantitative is always better” (it isn’t)
  • Introduction to “statistical discernment”: we will go deeper in Week 6, but the core idea: you do not need to compute statistics to understand what they mean

Activity: Match the Question to the Approach

Most of Monday is this. I hand out a set of research questions drawn from across CFANS, on paper, with no labels. In pairs, sort them.

  1. For each question, decide what the answer would have to look like. A number? A pattern across cases? An account of how something is experienced?
  2. From that, name the paradigm the question requires.
  3. Find the two questions you disagreed about most and write down why.
  4. Rewrite one question so it would require the other paradigm. What had to change?

Step four is the one that teaches. A question is not quantitative or qualitative because of its topic. It is one or the other because of what would count as an answer.

Discussion

  • What paradigm feels right for your question? Why?
  • Can anyone give an example of a question that could be answered with either paradigm, and how the answer would be different?

Wednesday: Research Question Workshop

Format: Workshop

Activity: Research Question Refinement

  1. Write your current research question on a notecard
  2. Apply a framework:
    • Quantitative: PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome)
    • Qualitative: “What is the experience/meaning/process of [X] for [population] in [context]?”
    • Mixed: Both, with a connecting logic
  3. Test your question against three criteria:
    • Is it answerable with evidence? (not a philosophical question)
    • Is it specific enough to design a study around?
    • Is it significant? (the “so what?” test: who cares and why?)
  4. Share with a partner. Partners ask: “What would you actually do to answer this?”
  5. Revise based on feedback

Whole-Class Share

Volunteers share their refined questions. Class discusses: What paradigm? What methods might work? What would the evidence look like?

Milestone Due

  • Progress Report 2: submitted on Canvas.

Supporting work for the next stage includes the refined research question and at least 15 sources in Zotero, organized with tags and at least five annotated.

Before Next Week

  • Review your research question, proposed method, and literature notes.
  • Bring one published result that is important to your project and be ready to explain what evidence supports it.

Fall Graduation Thesis Completion Track

Apply the frameworks to the thesis you are completing. Submit a Thesis Alignment Report showing whether the research question, completed work, methods, results, and claims still align. Name any claim the completed work can no longer support.
