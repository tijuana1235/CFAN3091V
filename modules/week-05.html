Week 5: Research Questions and What Counts as an Answer

Phase 2: Evidence and Methods

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish quantitative and qualitative research by what each one can establish, rather than by topic;
  • decide which approach your question requires, and say why;
  • refine a research question using PICO, meaning population, intervention, comparison, and outcome, or a structure better matched to the question you actually have;
  • judge whether a finding changes what you may conclude, or only what you know; and
  • state the significance of your question against the “so what?” test.

Purpose

Here is the failure I see most often in proposal drafts, and it is not a failure of effort. A student reads 20 papers, summarizes them accurately, and then writes a research question that none of those 20 papers would have changed. The reading happened. The inference did not move.

So this week runs on one question, and we will keep returning to it: does this knowledge actually change what you can conclude?

That question has teeth in both directions. A finding that contradicts your assumption should change your design, your question, or your expected result. A finding that confirms what you already believed should change how confidently you may assert it. If a source does neither, you have read it, but you have not used it. There is a difference between a fact that is interesting and a fact that is load-bearing, and a proposal that cannot tell them apart reads as a literature summary with a question stapled to the end.

You are not supposed to be good at this yet. Discerning which knowledge bears on an inference is a skill that takes practice, and it is the specific skill this week is built to develop.

Monday: What Would Count as an Answer?

Format: Focused instruction and a question-classification workshop.

Two Ways of Knowing

Research approaches are not ranked. They answer different questions, and they license different conclusions.

Two misconceptions worth naming directly, because both show up every year. “Qualitative is not rigorous” is false. Rigor is a property of design and execution, not of whether the evidence is numeric. “Quantitative is always stronger” is also false. A precise number answering the wrong question is worth less than a careful account answering the right one.

We will go deeper into what statistical results can and cannot license in Week 6, and that is also where mixed-methods designs belong, because they make the most sense once you have seen where a single approach runs out.

Activity: Match the Question to the Approach

Most of Monday is this. I hand out the Match the Question to the Approach worksheet: twelve research questions from across this college, on paper, with no labels. Copies are provided in class, and the printable version is on the assignment page if you would rather arrive with one you have already read. The questions were written for this exercise rather than taken from anyone’s thesis, so you can be blunt about them. In pairs, sort them.

  1. For each question, decide what the answer would have to look like. A number? A pattern across cases? An account of how something is experienced?
  2. From that, name the approach the question requires.
  3. Find the two questions you disagreed about most and write down why.
  4. Rewrite one question so it would require the other approach. What had to change?
  5. For that rewritten pair, name one conclusion the first version could support and the second could not.

Steps four and five are the ones that teach. A question is not quantitative or qualitative because of its topic. It is one or the other because of what would count as an answer. And when you change what would count as an answer, you change what you are entitled to conclude.

Discussion

  • What approach does your question require, and what would the answer look like?
  • Can anyone give a question that could be answered either way, and say how the two answers would differ in what they license?
  • Take your own question and answer this: what would have to change, either in the science or in what we currently know, before your conclusion would have to change? A new measurement? A result from a different system? An account from the people involved that contradicts the numbers? If you cannot name anything that would move you, the question is not yet doing work.

Wednesday: Refining the Question

Format: Workshop, on paper, with partner testing.

A vague question is not a modest question. It is a question that cannot fail, and a question that cannot fail cannot teach you anything. Refinement means adding enough structure that the question could come back with an answer you did not expect.

TipBreakout: What a Question That Cannot Fail Looks Like

Each of these is a real shape I see in Week 5 drafts. None of them is lazy. They are what a question looks like before it has been made specific.

Notice the pattern. Three of them fail because a key word is undefined, and three fail because they are true in advance. The repair is different in each case: define the term, or narrow the scope until a result could come back against you.

Structured Question Frameworks

A framework is a set of slots. Its value is not the acronym. Its value is that an empty slot is visible, and an empty slot is usually where the design problem is hiding.

The most familiar of these is PICO, which structures a question that compares one thing against another. It was developed for clinical questions about treatment, and it organizes a question around a patient or problem, an intervention or exposure, a comparison where one is relevant, and an outcome. Its stated value is procedural: filling the slots makes a question specific enough to guide which terms you search and where.

Two things about that are worth saying plainly, because you are being asked to use a tool built for a different discipline.

First, PICO comes from therapy-oriented clinical questions, and the review literature is explicit that it suits some information needs better than others (Eriksen and Frandsen 2018). It is not a universal template, and the next section is about what to do when it does not fit.

Second, whether PICO actually produces better searches than other structures, or than no structure at all, is not settled. A 2018 systematic review looking for evidence on exactly that question screened 2,163 records, assessed 22 full texts, and found only three eligible comparative studies. They varied so much in comparator, databases, and outcome measurement that no meta-analysis was possible, and the reviewers concluded that no solid practice implication could yet be drawn (Eriksen and Frandsen 2018). That is a statement about a thin evidence base, not a finding that PICO does not work.

So use it as what it is: a procedure for making an empty slot visible. That is a real and useful thing for a tool to do, and it does not require the tool to be proven superior. See the Evidence Boundary below for what this course does and does not claim about it.

Worked example. Start with a question a student could plausibly bring to me in Week 5:

Are cover crops good for water quality?

That question cannot fail. Every slot is empty. Filling them:

  • Population: continuous corn systems on tile-drained fields in southern Minnesota
  • Intervention: a cereal rye cover crop interseeded into standing corn
  • Comparison: a cover crop drilled after grain harvest
  • Outcome: nitrate-nitrogen concentration in tile drainage, in mg/L, across one drainage season

In tile-drained continuous corn in southern Minnesota, does cereal rye interseeded into standing corn reduce nitrate-nitrogen concentration in tile drainage compared with post-harvest drilled rye, over a single drainage season?

The second version can be wrong (i.e., a question can fail), which is what makes it worth asking. It also tells you what to search, what to measure, and what evidence would count.

When PICO Does Not Fit

Most thesis questions in this college are not intervention comparisons, and forcing them into PICO produces a worse question rather than a better one. If your question is descriptive, exploratory, or about meaning and process, the comparison slot will stay stubbornly empty. That is information, not failure.

TipBreakout: Watching a Question Break the Template

Take a question that is perfectly good and entirely unsuited to PICO:

How do wildlife technicians decide what to do when a field protocol does not fit the conditions in front of them?

Now force it into the four slots:

Two slots resist, and the damage shows up in the fourth. To fill Outcome you have to convert “how do they decide” into “how often do they deviate,” and that substitution quietly replaces a question about reasoning with a question about frequency. You would get a clean number and never learn what you wanted to know.

That is the tell. When filling a slot forces you to change what you are asking, stop filling slots and change the structure instead.

A related adaptation is worth knowing about, since some of you will work on exposures rather than interventions. In environmental, occupational, and public-health research, the same slot logic is adapted into PECO, replacing the intervention slot with an exposure (Morgan et al. 2018). That paper also makes the point this whole section rests on: no single framework is best everywhere, and the structure worth using depends on the kind of question being asked. If your question is about what a population is exposed to rather than what is done to it, that variant may sit closer to your work. Bring it to your Week 7 meeting if it applies.

Activity: Rebuild Your Question

Use the Rebuild Your Question worksheet. Copies are provided in class. Keep it when we finish; the rebuilt question goes into Progress Report 2.

  1. Write your current question at the top, exactly as you would say it out loud.
  2. Fill the slots from whichever structure fits it. Leave empty what is empty.
  3. Rewrite the question so it contains the filled slots.
  4. Test it against three standards:
    • Is it answerable with evidence you could actually collect?
    • Is it specific enough to design a study around?
    • Is it significant? Who cares, and why?
  5. Trade worksheets with a partner. Your partner asks two questions:
    • What would you actually do to answer this?
    • If the answer came back the opposite of what you expect, what in your proposal would change?

That second question is the inference test, and it is the one to sit with. It is the same test as the Monday discussion prompt, now aimed at your own proposal instead of someone else’s question.

Here is why it matters more than it looks. A proposal is an argument that some piece of work is worth doing, and the work is worth doing only if the result could land more than one way. So the test is not “is my question interesting?” It is “does my project have a stake in the answer?”

If nothing in your proposal would change, you are looking at one of two situations, and they need different repairs:

  • The question is not really open. You already believe the answer, and no plausible result would move you. The repair is to find the part you are genuinely uncertain about and ask that instead. It is often one layer down: not whether something works, but under what conditions, or for whom, or at what cost.
  • The proposal is not really resting on it. The question sits at the front of the document, but the methods, the design, and the expected results would proceed unchanged either way. The repair is structural. Either rebuild the design so it actually turns on the question, or admit the question you are really answering and write that one down.

Both are ordinary at this stage and both are cheap to repair in October. In November, when the document is drafted and your advisor is reading it, neither one is cheap. That is the whole reason this test happens now.

Whole-Class Share

We close by hearing a few questions before and after. Volunteering is genuinely optional, and a question that is still a mess is more useful to the room than a polished one, because the class gets to watch the repair happen rather than just admire the result.

For each one, we work out together: what approach does it now require, what evidence would count as an answer, and what does the refined version rule out that the original did not? That last one is the payoff. A question that rules nothing out has not been refined, however much longer it has become.

Evidence Boundary

I ask you all term to say what your evidence supports and where it runs out, so it would be poor form not to do the same for the material I hand you.

Here is where this page stands.

What is supported. PICO’s four-part structure, its origin in clinical questions about treatment, and the point that its value is procedural rather than proven all come from a 2018 systematic review (Eriksen and Frandsen 2018). So does the harder statement: that review went looking for evidence that PICO improves search quality, found three eligible studies, judged them heterogeneous and at high or unclear risk of bias, and drew no practice implications. The PECO adaptation and the principle that no single framework fits every question come from a methodological framework paper in environmental health (Morgan et al. 2018), which is a commentary rather than a study that measured anything.

What is not. No source in this course’s evidence review shows that teaching a question framework improves student research questions, proposals, or theses. That is a different claim from the ones above, and nobody has established it for this setting. The non-comparative slot table in this section is my own adaptation for the kinds of questions students actually bring me. It is not taken from a validated instrument, and no study backs it.

What that means for you. Use the frameworks as procedures that make an empty slot visible, which is a modest and honest description of what they do. Do not cite this page as evidence that structured questions produce better research. If your own thesis needs a claim about question frameworks, go to the literature yourself rather than borrowing my framing. Both sources above are in the reference list at the end of this page, and both are worth ten minutes if you are building a methods argument.

The Week 5 lecture-evidence records stay marked as gaps until instruction-level evidence is reviewed and approved, which is a deliberate record of what is missing rather than an oversight.
