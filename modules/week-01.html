Learning Objectives

By the end of our one meeting this week, you should be able to:

  • describe the purpose of the course, identify your likely course track, and name the major professional-review and completion dates, recognizing that program requirements and graduation term may change which dates apply;
  • explain why a paper’s topic, research question, central claim, and supporting evidence are different things;
  • follow an instructor-led paper dissection and record a first, tentative claim-and-evidence entry; and
  • begin exploring the interests, motivations, and possible directions that will feed next week’s topic-funnel work.

You are not expected to dissect a research article independently after one class. This first pass gives us shared language and a procedure we will reuse and strengthen throughout the semester.

Part 3: Taking One Paper Apart Together

Everyone works from the same paper. I will lead the dissection at the board, and we will complete the Week 1 Literature Intake Form together. The form page includes a two-page printable PDF, and printed copies will be available. Keep the handout and your marked-up paper. They are the first entries in the claims-and-evidence record you will build across the course.

Why Build a Claims Ledger?

A reference list tells you what you found. A claims ledger records what you may use from it, why the evidence supports that use, and where the limits are. Building the ledger while the paper is in front of you saves you from trying to reconstruct the authors’ reasoning weeks later. It also helps you:

  • locate the exact passage, result, figure, or table behind a statement;
  • distinguish an author’s evidence from your own interpretation;
  • compare claims that agree, conflict, or apply under different conditions;
  • notice where several papers repeat a claim without adding new evidence; and
  • draft literature-review paragraphs around patterns in the evidence rather than writing one summary per article.

The paper form is the first entry, not the required long-term technology. You can maintain the same structure in several ways:

  • In Zotero: Create a child note for a source with fixed headings such as Claim, Evidence and location, Connection, Boundary, and Status. Add a claim-ledger tag plus topic or method tags. When several sources address the same issue, shared tags let you retrieve them as a group.
  • In a spreadsheet: Use one row per claim, not one row per article. Useful columns include citation key, page or figure, claim, evidence, connection, limitation, topic, and status. One paper may therefore occupy several rows.
  • In a database: Import the spreadsheet as claim-source records connected to a source table by a stable source ID or Zotero citation key. A more developed system can keep separate source, claim, and evidence-link tables. That structure lets one source support several claims and one claim connect to evidence from several sources without duplicating the full citation.

For example, you might filter the ledger to find every claim tagged weed-management, group those entries by experimental design, and then compare which conclusions hold across sites. Or you might retrieve every entry marked needs closer reading before drafting so an uncertain interpretation does not quietly become a confident sentence. We will start on paper so the reasoning is visible; later, you may transfer the entries into the system that best supports your project.

NoteReading for this session

Davis, A. S. (2010). Cover-crop roller-crimper contributes to weed management in no-till soybean. Weed Science, 58(3), 300-309. https://doi.org/10.1614/WS-D-09-00040.1

This is a guided structural pass, not a test and not yet a critical appraisal. Published reading guides recommend reading with a purpose, previewing the article’s structure before reading closely, taking notes, and returning to the paper more than once. They also warn against treating the standard empirical article structure as universal: fields without conventional Methods and Results sections still have ways of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting evidence (Carey et al. 2020; George Mason University Libraries n.d.).

Our first pass adapts that guidance into four moves:

  1. Preview. Inspect the title, abstract, headings, figures, and tables. Write down what you expect the paper to address, but treat that expectation as provisional.
  2. Orient. Locate the problem or gap and state the research question in your own words. Point to the passage that supports your interpretation.
  3. Trace. Select one consequential claim, then trace it to a specific result, observation, figure, table, passage, or other evidence. Inspect the method that produced that evidence.
  4. Bound. Name what the selected evidence does not establish and what you would inspect on a closer reading.

We will record candidate answers, point to where they appear, and name what we are still uncertain about. “Candidate” matters. A first reading can produce a plausible interpretation that changes when we inspect the paper more closely.

The reading protocol gives you a starting procedure for empirical research articles. It is a heuristic, not a universal map. Different disciplines organize questions and evidence differently, and we will adapt the procedure as those differences appear.

Part 4: Your Work for the Rest of the Week

Interest, Motivation, and Direction Freewrite

Do not search for three faculty members yet. First, give yourself something worth searching from.

Write informally about:

  1. the problems, systems, questions, or experiences that hold your attention;
  2. why those interests matter to you;
  3. what motivates you to investigate or work on them;
  4. the kinds of activities or work you enjoy doing;
  5. a role, job, profession, or career that might involve that kind of work;
  6. what you already know and what you would need to explore; and
  7. one uncertainty you want help thinking through.

This is exploratory writing. You do not need a settled thesis topic, a research question, or polished prose. Spend enough time to produce material you can use in Monday’s topic-funnel exercise.

Use the accessible assignment sheet or download the printable PDF. Bring one printed copy to class on Monday, September 14. Use Times New Roman, 12 point, double spacing, one-inch margins, continuous line numbers, and single-sided printing. You will use the freewrite in the topic-funnel activity and hand it in at the end of class. The result will appear in Canvas, but there is no routine Canvas upload. This is the second participation component of Milestone 1. It is evaluated against the exploratory standard on the assignment sheet, not for technical polish.

Fall Graduation Thesis Completion Track

Use the same prompts through the thesis you are already completing. Write about why the project mattered, how it connects to work or a future direction, what still motivates you, what you have learned about the work you want to do, and the most important uncertainty or threat to completion. We will address the formal thesis-status and deadline audit in the planning sequence that follows.
