Week 6: Statistical Discernment and Mixed Methods

Phase 2: Evidence and Methods

Week at a Glance

This week
Learn what a statistical result does and does not license, then audit two papers from your own field.
Before class
For Monday, nothing new. For Wednesday, choose two printed primary research papers from your field against the criteria below.
Bring
Monday: your marked Week 1 article, Davis (2010), and the reading protocol. Wednesday: your two printed papers and two printed copies of the Claim Worksheet, one per paper.
Due
Method and Feasibility Blueprint on Wednesday, October 14. Fall Graduation students submit the Thesis Method, Analysis, and Feasibility Audit instead. Section 7 of the Blueprint is where the published result you selected in Week 5 gets narrowed; on the Audit, Section 6 does the same work on a claim of your own.
Submission method
Hand in the printed form for your track at the end of Wednesday’s class. The Blueprint is a design memo rather than a worksheet: the printable form gives you the headings and prompts, and you may type it instead using the same headings.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish a research claim from the evidence offered for it, and identify the population, comparison, outcome, and design standing behind it;
  • state what a p-value does and does not measure, and separate statistical significance from practical importance;
  • distinguish association from causation, and judge whether a paper’s own wording stays inside what its design supports;
  • explain why selective analysis and reporting inflate the published record, and name what a reader can check;
  • read a figure or table for what it hides as well as what it shows; and
  • say why a mixed-methods design would or would not serve your question.

Monday: What a Result Does and Does Not License

Format: Focused instruction, then guided practice on a shared paper.

Statistical discernment begins before calculation. A result is interpretable only in relation to the question, design, population, measurement, and uncertainty that produced it.

That caution is not merely stylistic. Hill and colleagues (Hill et al. 2024) ran a randomized experiment in an introductory statistics course, giving students short written vignettes reporting a finding and asking how strongly they read it as causal. Both the wording of the finding and its topic affected their answers, and associational language by itself did not reliably prevent a causal reading. This study does not test our class activity, but it gives us a concrete reason to inspect the sentence and the study design together.

Probability, and What a P-Value Actually Is

You do not need to compute a p-value to read one, but you do need to know what it is a probability of, because that is where nearly every misreading starts.

Start with the picture, because the arithmetic follows from it.

Both curves are expectations, not data. Each one says: if this were the true state of the world, here is the spread of results you would expect to see. The left curve assumes no effect. The right curve assumes a real one.

Now look at where the curves overlap, because that region is the entire problem. The observed result sits inside it. It is an unremarkable outcome under the right-hand curve and an unusual one under the left, but it is a possible outcome under both. No amount of arithmetic changes that.

The p-value measures one thing on this picture: the shaded area, meaning how much of the no-effect curve sits at or beyond your result. A small p-value says your data would be surprising if there were no effect. It does not say how much of the right-hand curve sits there, and it never tells you which curve you are actually standing in.

TipBreakout: What “Probability” Means Here, With One Die

Before p-values, settle what a probability is a statement about.

A probability is a statement about a setup, not about a result you already have. Take a fair six-sided die. The probability of rolling a 4 is one in six, or about 0.17. That number describes the die and the throw. It does not describe any particular roll, and the moment the die lands the probability is gone: it either is a 4 or it is not.

So probability runs one direction, from the setup to the outcome. Given this die, how often would I see this result?

Now roll three sixes in a row. If the die is fair, the chance of that is one in 216, about 0.005. That is a small number, and it is a p-value in miniature: if the fair-die assumption held, this run would be surprising.

Here is the step almost everyone takes and should not. That 0.005 is not the probability that the die is loaded. It cannot be, because we never asked how often loaded dice turn up in the first place. If every die in the room is fair, three sixes is just a rare evening. If half the dice are loaded, the same run means something else entirely. The rolls alone cannot tell you which room you are in.

That is the whole misreading, in a form you can hold in your hand. The p-value answers “how surprising are these data if the assumption held?” People hear it answer “how likely is the assumption?” Those are different questions, and the second one needs information the data do not contain.

A statistical model is a set of assumptions about how the data could have come about. A p-value is computed under such a model, including the assumption that there is no effect, otherwise known as the null hypothesis. It reports how unusual data like yours would be if those assumptions held. In the words of the American Statistical Association’s formal statement on the subject, “P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model” (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016).

Now the direction, which is the whole game. The p-value runs from the model to the data: given these assumptions, how surprising are these data? It does not run from the data back to the truth of the hypothesis. The ASA states this directly: p-values “do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone” (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016).

ImportantWhat the ASA Statement Says

In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement on statistical significance and p-values, written for researchers and practitioners who are not primarily statisticians. It sets out six principles, in the statement’s own words (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016):

  1. “P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.”
  2. “P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.”
  3. “Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.”
  4. “Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.”
  5. “A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.”
  6. “By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis.”

Read those carefully, because they are more limited than the arguments people build on top of them. The statement does not say p-values are invalid; principle one affirms a legitimate use. It does not propose a replacement threshold. It notes that some statisticians prefer other approaches, including estimation with intervals and Bayesian methods, while saying those “rely on further assumptions” rather than endorsing them as superior. And the ASA is careful that none of this is new: “Statisticians and others have been sounding the alarm about these matters for decades, to little avail.”

The conclusion is the sentence to carry into your own proposal: “No single index should substitute for scientific reasoning.”

Notice what principle five gives you. Significance and magnitude are different questions, and a paper can answer the first while leaving the second unaddressed. A yield difference of 0.2 Mg/ha can be highly significant with enough replication and still be too small for a grower to notice. Significance answers “is it distinguishable from noise?” Magnitude answers “does it matter?” You need both, and only one of them is usually printed in bold.

Selective Analysis and the Published Record

Principle four is the one with the sharpest edge for you as a reader. Proper inference “requires full reporting and transparency,” and the ASA names the practices that violate it: “data dredging, significance chasing, significance questing, selective inference.” Their consequence is stated plainly. Such practice “leads to a spurious excess of statistically significant results in the published literature and should be vigorously avoided” (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016).

Sit with that phrase, “a spurious excess in the published literature,” because it is a claim about the shelf you are building your source set from. The individual analyses can each look defensible while the visible record tilts.

Here is the mechanism. Any real analysis involves choices: which observations to exclude, which outcome to treat as primary, which covariates to include, when to stop collecting. Make those choices after seeing which ones produce a significant result, and you will find significance more often than the stated error rate implies. This does not require dishonesty. It requires only that the choices be made in the presence of the results.

What you can do about it as a reader is narrower than you might hope, and it is worth being honest about that. You cannot detect this from a single p-value; the number looks the same either way. What you can ask is whether the analysis plan was specified in advance, how many outcomes and comparisons were examined against how many were reported, whether the primary outcome in the methods is the one featured in the abstract, and whether the reported result stands alone or sits in a pattern the authors show you.

Claim-Inspection Protocol

For each result, ask:

  1. What exactly is being claimed?
  2. Who or what was studied?
  3. What comparison produced the result?
  4. What was measured, and how?
  5. How much uncertainty surrounds the estimate, and what is that uncertainty a statement about?
  6. Does the design support the conclusion?
  7. Is the difference meaningful in the research context?

Questions five and seven are the ones the material above equips. Before this week, “how much uncertainty” was a box to tick. You can now say what the number in that box is a probability of, and question seven is the one no statistic answers for you.

Guided Practice

The rest of Monday stays on the Week 1 article. You already know this paper, which means you can spend the hour on the inspection instead of on orientation.

I bring three claims about the Davis results and put them on the board. They are my sentences, not the authors’, and they do not all hold up. Your job is to run the seven questions on each one and decide what the printed results actually entitle that sentence to say. We work the first as a class, you work the second in pairs, and we compare answers on the third.

Expect to disagree about question seven. Whether an effect is large enough to matter is not a statistical question, and two people reading the same table can reasonably land in different places. That is the point of doing it out loud.

Mixed Methods: Designing for What Neither Approach Settles

Recall from Week 5 that quantitative and qualitative work license different conclusions, and that each has a row in the table listing what it cannot establish alone. Now you have seen a concrete instance. A significant difference in tile nitrate tells you the magnitude of a change. It tells you nothing about why some growers adopted the practice and others did not, and a proposal that needs both is not served by choosing harder.

That is what a mixed-methods design is for. It is not “both, because more is better,” and two studies stapled together in one document is not a mixed-methods design. The defining feature is a connecting logic: a stated reason the two strands are in the same study, and a named point where they meet.

Three common connecting logics, as we will use the term in this course:

  • One strand explains the other. Measure the effect, then use interviews or cases to account for the variation the numbers show.
  • One strand builds the other. Use exploratory work to find out what the right questions and categories even are, then measure at scale.
  • The strands test the same claim from different angles. Where they agree, confidence rises. Where they disagree, that disagreement is a finding and belongs in the write-up rather than in a footnote.

The design question to answer in your Blueprint is not “should I do mixed methods.” It is: what does my question need that one strand cannot deliver, and where exactly do the strands meet? If you cannot name the meeting point, you have two studies and one deadline.

Wednesday: Evidence Detectives

Bring two papers from your own field and audit them. You are not recomputing anything. You are working out what the authors are entitled to say.

What to Bring

Two papers, printed, that meet all of these:

  • primary research, not a review or a commentary;
  • reports numbers of some kind, whether means, counts, frequencies, scores, or participants;
  • includes at least one figure or table of results; and
  • close enough to your own question that you would consider citing it.

The numbers requirement is not a quantitative-only requirement. An interview study reports how many people were approached, how many participated, and how many raised a given theme. A sensory panel reports scores. Those are the numbers this audit is about.

Print them. You will be marking up displays and circling numbers, and the audit does not work if you are reading over someone’s shoulder at a laptop.

The Audit

Three questions do most of the work, and all three are answerable without software:

  1. How many? Find the number, and find where it shrank. Studies report how many were enrolled, approached, or planted, then analyze a smaller number. The gap is where problems hide. Ask whether that final number supports a claim about a population, or only about the units actually measured. This question does not change between designs. A theme reported by three of 22 interviewees and written up as “participants described” has the same problem as a yield mean with four surviving plots.
  2. How much? Find the size of the effect and its units, separately from any p-value. Significance answers “is it real?” Effect size answers “does it matter?” You need both. Where the work is not reporting effects, ask the parallel question: how much of the evidence is this claim resting on?
  3. What is the display not showing? A bar chart of means hides the spread. A table of percentages hides its denominator. A table of means hides the variation just as completely as the bar chart does. Look for whether you can see the variation, the individual observations, or the uncertainty. If you cannot, ask why not.

Then one check carried over from Monday: what does this paper let you find out about how its analysis was chosen? Look for whether the analysis plan was specified in advance, whether the primary outcome named in the methods is the one featured in the abstract, and how many outcomes or comparisons were examined against how many were reported. Often you will not be able to tell. Write that down. “Cannot determine” is the finding, and it is the reason principle four asks for full reporting.

Group Report

Complete a Claim Worksheet for each paper, then report out on one of them:

  • the strongest conclusion the evidence supports;
  • one conclusion the evidence does not support;
  • how many units were actually analyzed, and what that limits the claim to;
  • whether the effect or pattern is large enough to matter in that research context;
  • what the figures and tables conceal about variation;
  • whether the paper’s own wording is associational or causal, and whether the design supports the version it uses;
  • what the paper lets you check about how its analysis was chosen, and what it does not;
  • one design or measurement limitation;
  • one question you would ask the authors; and
  • whether and how you could use the result in a proposal.

The worksheet allows “not reported” and “cannot determine.” Use them. A question the paper does not let you answer is a finding about the paper, not a blank you failed to fill.

Across the room, expect at least one paper where the statistics are sound and the sentence describing them is not. That gap, between what was found and what was said, is the thing you are training to see. Expect at least one paper that says exactly what its evidence supports, too. Recognizing that is the same skill running in the other direction, and it is the reason this course is not teaching you to distrust everything you read.

Evidence Boundary

The p-value and reporting material on this page is attributed to the American Statistical Association’s formal statement, which is an authoritative professional position rather than an empirical study (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016). It supports claims about what a p-value measures and about full reporting. It does not support any claim about how these ideas should be taught, and no such claim is made here.

The mixed-methods connecting logics, the claim-inspection protocol, and the audit procedure are course procedures. The course evidence review has not verified a source for claims about mixed-methods instruction, so this page does not present those procedures as evidence-verified. The Week 6 lecture-evidence records carry that gap as a required action on both the Monday and the Wednesday session; what those records do verify is narrower, and is the causal-language finding cited above.

Optional Reading

For a readable account of how these debates play out in practice, written for a general audience by a statistician working as a journalist, see Nuzzo (2014) in Nature (Nuzzo 2014). It is science journalism rather than peer-reviewed research, so treat it as an orientation to the conversation rather than as evidence. It is also the source of a line worth keeping: Steven Goodman, quoted there, that “the numbers are where the scientific discussion should start, not end.”
