Week 6: Statistical Discernment

Phase 2: Evidence and Methods

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish a research claim from the evidence offered for it;
  • identify the population, comparison, outcome, and design behind a claim;
  • distinguish association from causation;
  • distinguish statistical significance from practical importance;
  • read a figure for what it hides as well as what it shows; and
  • identify questions that must be answered before using a result in your own proposal.

Monday: Reading Claims in Context

Statistical discernment begins before calculation. A result is interpretable only in relation to the question, design, population, measurement, and uncertainty that produced it.

That caution is not merely stylistic. In an introductory-statistics vignette experiment, both the wording of a finding and its topic affected students’ causal interpretations; using associational language by itself did not reliably prevent a causal reading (Hill et al. 2024). This study does not test our class activity, but it gives us a concrete reason to inspect the sentence and the study design together.

Reading Clinic: The Evaluative Pass

Fifteen minutes, on the Week 1 article, with the reading protocol in front of you.

Week 1 was a structural pass: what does this paper say, and where. This is the second pass the protocol promised. Take the conclusion you underlined in Week 1 and ask a harder question about it: how many of the seven inspection questions below can you actually answer from what the authors printed?

You will not answer all seven. The ones you cannot answer are the finding.

Claim-Inspection Protocol

For each result, ask:

  1. What exactly is being claimed?
  2. Who or what was studied?
  3. What comparison produced the result?
  4. What was measured, and how?
  5. How much uncertainty surrounds the estimate?
  6. Does the design support the conclusion?
  7. Is the difference meaningful in the research context?

Guided Practice

The rest of Monday stays on the Week 1 article. You already know this paper, which means you can spend the hour on the inspection instead of on orientation.

I bring three claims about the Davis results and put them on the board. They are my sentences, not the authors’, and they do not all hold up. Your job is to run the seven questions on each one and decide what the printed results actually entitle that sentence to say. We work the first as a class, you work the second in pairs, and we compare answers on the third.

Expect to disagree about question seven. Whether an effect is large enough to matter is not a statistical question, and two people reading the same table can reasonably land in different places. That is the point of doing it out loud.

Wednesday: Evidence Detectives

Bring two papers from your own field and audit them. You are not recomputing anything. You are working out what the authors are entitled to say.

What to Bring

Two papers, printed, that meet all of these:

  • primary research, not a review or a commentary;
  • reports numbers of some kind, whether means, counts, frequencies, scores, or participants;
  • includes at least one figure or table of results; and
  • close enough to your own question that you would consider citing it.

The numbers requirement is not a quantitative-only requirement. An interview study reports how many people were approached, how many participated, and how many raised a given theme. A sensory panel reports scores. Those are the numbers this audit is about.

Print them. You will be marking up displays and circling numbers, and the audit does not work if you are reading over someone’s shoulder at a laptop.

The Audit

Three questions do most of the work, and all three are answerable without software:

  1. How many? Find the number, and find where it shrank. Studies report how many were enrolled, approached, or planted, then analyze a smaller number. The gap is where problems hide. Ask whether that final number supports a claim about a population, or only about the units actually measured. This question does not change between designs. A theme reported by three of 22 interviewees and written up as “participants described” has the same problem as a yield mean with four surviving plots.
  2. How much? Find the size of the effect and its units, separately from any p-value. A yield difference of 0.2 Mg/ha can be highly significant with enough replication and still be too small for a grower to notice. Significance answers “is it real?” Effect size answers “does it matter?” You need both. Where the work is not reporting effects, ask the parallel question: how much of the evidence is this claim resting on?
  3. What is the display not showing? A bar chart of means hides the spread. A table of percentages hides its denominator. A table of means hides the variation just as completely as the bar chart does. Look for whether you can see the variation, the individual observations, or the uncertainty. If you cannot, ask why not.

Group Report

Complete a claim worksheet for each paper, then report out on one of them:

  • the strongest conclusion the evidence supports;
  • one conclusion the evidence does not support;
  • how many units were actually analyzed, and what that limits the claim to;
  • whether the effect or pattern is large enough to matter in that research context;
  • what the figures and tables conceal about variation;
  • one design or measurement limitation;
  • one question you would ask the authors; and
  • whether and how you could use the result in a proposal.

The worksheet allows “not reported” and “cannot determine.” Use them. A question the paper does not let you answer is a finding about the paper, not a blank you failed to fill.

Across the room, expect at least one paper where the statistics are sound and the sentence describing them is not. That gap, between what was found and what was said, is the thing you are training to see. Expect at least one paper that says exactly what its evidence supports, too. Recognizing that is the same skill running in the other direction, and it is the reason this course is not teaching you to distrust everything you read.

Milestone

Submit the Method and Feasibility Blueprint. Connect the research question to the unit or cases, evidence or data, collection and analysis plan, feasibility constraints, ethics or permissions when applicable, the largest design risk, a viable alternative, a calibrated claim, and one question for your advisor. See Written Assignments for what counts as satisfactory.

Fall Graduation Thesis Completion Track

Apply the claim-inspection protocol to your own results, figures, or analysis. Submit a Thesis Method, Analysis, and Feasibility Audit identifying the strongest supported claim, at least one claim that needs qualification, any analysis or visual still required, the largest completion risk, and a feasible alternative if the current plan cannot be completed before professional review.

Before Wednesday

Choose your two papers against the criteria above and print them. Bring the claim worksheet linked on the course site.

If you cannot find two that fit, bring what you have and say so at the start of class. A paper that turns out to be a review, or that reports no numbers you can locate, is worth five minutes of the room’s attention rather than a quiet substitution. Spare papers are available if you arrive without one, though your own field is where this skill has to land.
