Week 12: Figures, Tables, and Abstracts

Phase 3: Proposal Development

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • design a figure with labeled axes, units, a legend, and no decoration that carries no information;
  • build a table whose headers, alignment, and ordering are readable linearly;
  • write a caption self-contained enough that the figure survives being read on its own;
  • distinguish a table caption from a figure legend and write each one using the information a reader needs; and
  • draft a 250 to 300 word abstract covering context, gap, objective, method, and expected contribution.

Monday: Figures, Tables, and Abstracts

Format: Short clinics + proposal assembly workshop

Topics

  • General principles: clarity, consistency, accuracy, simplicity
  • Figure types and when to use each:
    • Bar charts, scatter plots, box plots for quantitative data
    • Conceptual diagrams, flow charts for frameworks and processes
    • Maps for spatial research
  • Table structure: row headers, column headers, alignment, units, meaningful ordering
  • Writing table captions and figure legends: descriptive, self-contained, and concise enough that a reader can interpret the visual without searching the body text
  • Common mistakes: distorted or truncated scales, 3-D effects, dual axes that invite false comparisons, rainbow color scales, missing units or denominators, unexplained abbreviations, excessive precision, chartjunk, and repeating the same information in prose and a visual
  • Tools: R/ggplot2, Excel, BioRender (for conceptual diagrams), draw.io (for flowcharts)
  • Abstract structure: context → gap → objective → methods → expected contribution (250–300 words)

Examples

We will compare published examples of effective and misleading visual design. The point is not to mock a graphic; it is to identify the decision that makes the visual harder to interpret. We will use this discussion of truncated axes, this statistical-figure reporting guidance, and these caption examples.

The Stand-Alone Test

“Stand alone” does not mean that a figure or table needs no connection to the argument. It means that a reader who encounters the visual apart from the paragraph that first introduced it can still determine what was measured or shown, for whom or what, under what conditions, and how to read every label, symbol, abbreviation, and uncertainty mark. The visual should add information; the prose should explain why that information matters.

Use this test: cover the surrounding paragraphs and ask, What is this, who or what does it describe, what do the numbers or marks mean, and what comparison should I make? If the reader cannot answer, repair the visual or its caption/legend before polishing the prose.

Caption and Legend Recipe

The table caption goes above the table. The figure legend goes below the figure. Both begin with a number and a specific title, not a vague label such as “Results.” Then include the information needed to interpret the object:

  1. What: the variable, relationship, process, or comparison shown.
  2. Who or what: the population, system, cases, samples, or documents.
  3. How: the design, time point, grouping, or calculation that produced it.
  4. How to read it: units, denominators, symbols, colors, line types, abbreviations, and uncertainty or statistical notation.
  5. Source and boundary: the data source, adaptation credit, and any limit that changes what the reader may conclude.

Example table caption: Table 1. Mean soybean yield and weed biomass by treatment in the field comparison. Values are means across four plots per treatment; yield is reported in Mg ha−1 and weed biomass in g m−2. Error bars are not applicable because this table reports means only. Adapted from the study protocol described in the methods.

Example figure legend: Figure 1. Expected relationship between cover-crop biomass and soybean yield under the proposed field comparison. Each point represents one plot; the horizontal axis is cover-crop biomass (g m−2), and the vertical axis is soybean yield (Mg ha−1). The shaded band marks the range of outcomes the proposal treats as practically acceptable, not a confidence interval.

Do not put a conclusion in place of a description. The caption tells the reader what the visual contains and how to interpret it; the paragraph tells the reader what the pattern means for the research question.

A Short ggplot2 Clinic

Use ggplot2 only if it serves your project. This is a 15-minute reproducible plotting clinic, not a programming unit. The transferable sequence is data → mapping → geometry → scales → labels → caption:

ggplot(data, aes(x = treatment, y = outcome, colour = group)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(
    x = "Treatment",
    y = "Outcome (units)",
    colour = "Group",
    caption = "Source: ...; points represent ..."
  ) +
  theme_classic()

Students using Excel or another tool apply the same checks: map the correct variables, label the full variable names and units, use a legend only when it decodes information, and record the source and uncertainty. The official ggplot2 labels documentation and guide documentation are references for labels and legends, not required reading.

Assembly Begins Monday

The clinics are short on purpose. The second half of Monday starts the assembly: put your sections in order, read the seams between them, and list what is missing. Wednesday finishes that work and tests it on a partner.

Starting Monday matters because the gaps you find are usually not sentence problems. They are a missing method detail or a claim with no source behind it, and those take more than one session to close.

Whole-Document Review and Release Screen

Format: Workshop

Upload the complete working Advisor-ready Full Thesis Proposal and required release materials to Canvas before class on Monday, Nov 23, which is Milestone 12a. This is the draft you submit for the instructor’s full-document review. The exchange below is an informal whole-document critique, not one of the two graded student peer reviews. Peer Review 2 happened in Week 11. The purpose today is to test whether the document works as one readable argument without the writer supplying missing connections.

Your working draft should incorporate the highest-priority Peer Review 2 edits you can complete in the available time. It may still contain unresolved comments; identify those decisions in the Whole-Document Revision Memo. The instructor review focuses on the complete argument and the three revisions that matter most, not line-level editing of every sentence.

Activity

  1. Exchange the complete document with a peer.
  2. Trace the question, literature-based rationale, method, outcome or expected outcome, and claimed contribution through the entire document.
  3. Test the figure or table and caption without relying on the surrounding prose.
  4. Mark every missing connection, unsupported claim, unresolved feasibility issue, and unmet program requirement.
  5. Complete the one-page Whole-Document Revision Memo, naming the three highest-priority revisions and the evidence for each.
  6. Complete the instructor release screen against the core final-document rubric.

Peer Feedback on Visuals

Use the Visual Audit and Caption Clinic worksheet. For each visual, answer:

  • Is it necessary, and does it add information beyond the text?
  • Can a reader identify the population, variables, units, denominators, and comparison without the surrounding prose?
  • Are the scale, ordering, and encodings honest and readable?
  • Are symbols, abbreviations, uncertainty marks, and data sources explained?
  • Is the table caption above the table or the figure legend below the figure?
  • Does the paragraph interpret the visual instead of repeating every value?

Wednesday: Release Revisions and Professional-Review Send

Use Wednesday to complete the bounded release revisions, including the highest-priority issues from Peer Review 2, the whole-document exchange, and the instructor review. Assemble the released version and send it to the Primary Advisor and/or Proposal Committee no later than the end of the day on Nov 25, then submit the confirmation as Milestone 12b. Record the delivery without submitting private correspondence.

Essential Milestone Due

The Week 12 milestone contains the Advisor-ready Full Thesis Proposal, Whole-Document Review, Revision Memo, Release Screen, and professional-review submission. Every part must be satisfactory. This milestone is essential for passing the course.

See Week 12: Advisor-ready Document and Release Screen for the advisor-ready standard, which is not the same as finished or polished.

Apply the visual and abstract clinics to actual findings, complete the same whole-document review and release screen, then send the released Advisor-ready Full Thesis Draft for final professional review by Nov 25.
