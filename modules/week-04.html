Week 4: Literature Search and Source Evaluation

Phase 1: Foundations

Week at a Glance

This week
Build a documented database search on Monday, then grow one useful source into an 11-source citation network on Wednesday.
Before class
Install Zotero and its browser connector, confirm that you can reach the University Libraries, and bring your current research question or focused problem.
Bring
A laptop and charger, your marked Week 1 article, the reading protocol, and the Week 4 Search Workbook.
Due
Primary Advisor Identification or the Literature and Reference Audit on Wednesday, September 30.
Submission method
Hand in the Week 4 milestone on paper. Keep the Search Workbook; it becomes the first draft of the Search Record due in Week 5.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  • translate a research question into searchable concept groups;
  • choose a database or disciplinary collection based on its coverage;
  • revise a query by recording what the results reveal;
  • distinguish a review, a primary study, and gray literature by the work each source does;
  • organize sources in Zotero with verified metadata, consistent tags, and usable notes; and
  • trace a useful source backward and forward through its citation network.

Purpose

A large result count is not a literature base. It is only a result count. The work this week is to make the path inspectable: what you searched, where you searched, what changed, why a source stayed, and how one useful source led to another.

ImportantBring a Laptop This Week

Week 4 is the course’s screen-based exception because databases and Zotero are the subject of the instruction. Both sessions require a laptop and charger.

If you do not have a suitable device or cannot install Zotero, tell me before Monday. We will arrange an accessible course device or a browser-based fallback before class begins. Do not spend the workshop fighting an access problem in silence.

Monday: Build a Search You Can Explain

Format: Short demonstrations followed by guided work in your own field.

Use the Week 4 Search Workbook throughout the session. This is working documentation, not a polished product.

1. Move from a Question to Concepts

Most of you have never run a search in a library database, and a good number of you have never taken a logic course. You are not supposed to know this yet. That is why we are spending a full session on it, on paper first and at the keyboard second.

A database does not read your question. It matches the strings you hand it against the fields it has indexed. So the first move is not typing. It is taking the question apart.

Copy your current question or focused problem. Underline the parts that a database could index: a population or system, a process or exposure, an outcome, a place, a period, a method, or a type of source. Not every project needs the same categories.

TipBreakout: Where Keywords Actually Come From

You do not invent keywords. You harvest them from work that already exists, which is why we start from a paper you have already read rather than from a blank page.

Five places to harvest, in the order they usually pay off:

  1. The title. Authors compress their topic into about a dozen words. Those words are load-bearing.
  2. The abstract. Read it for nouns, not for argument. The population, the thing done, and the thing measured are usually all there.
  3. The author keywords. Printed under the abstract in most journals. These are the terms the authors expect searchers to use.
  4. The subject headings or descriptors. Assigned by the database, not the author, and visible on the record page. One good record hands you the controlled vocabulary of an entire field.
  5. The introduction’s framing sentences. The terms an author uses to say what is already known are the terms already circulating in that literature.

A caution about the fourth item. Subject headings are powerful precisely because they are standardized, and standardization is field-specific. Health databases and agriculture databases can index the same study under different headings. Harvest from the database you are actually in.

TipBreakout: AND, OR, and NOT Before They Are Search Syntax

Forget databases for a minute. These three words are about sets: collections of things.

Picture every student in this room. Now picture two overlapping groups: the ones who have declared a major in the college, and the ones graduating in December.

  • OR is the union. “In the college OR graduating in December” gathers everyone in either group. Every person from both circles. OR makes the result larger.
  • AND is the intersection. “In the college AND graduating in December” keeps only the people in both groups at once. AND makes the result smaller.
  • NOT is exclusion. “In the college NOT graduating in December” keeps the first group and removes anyone who is also in the second.

Here is the part that trips people, every year, in every field. OR feels restrictive because the English word “or” often means “one or the other, pick one.” In a search it means “either counts, give me both.” AND feels generous because “and” sounds additive. In a search it is the strict one: every term you add with AND is another condition a record must satisfy simultaneously, and each one throws results away.

So the shape of a working search is: OR inside a concept, AND between concepts.

(cover crop OR "green manure" OR rye)    <- concept 1: synonyms, joined by OR
AND
(weed OR "weed suppression")             <- concept 2: synonyms, joined by OR

Alternate terms for one idea go inside one set of parentheses joined by OR, because any of them would be an acceptable hit. Different ideas are joined by AND, because a useful record has to be about both. The parentheses are not decoration. Without them the database applies the operators in its own default order and you get a result set you cannot explain.

Two mechanical notes that cause more failed searches than any concept: quotation marks hold a phrase together, so "cover crop" searches the phrase and cover crop may search the two words separately; and a truncation symbol, usually *, catches word endings, so weed* retrieves weed, weeds, and weedy. Both are conveniences with a cost. * placed too early, as in wee*, will hand you week, weekly, and weep.

Use NOT sparingly. It removes records permanently from your result set, including the ones that mention your unwanted term once, in passing, while being exactly what you needed.

Worked Exercise: Build a Keyword Table from the Davis Paper

We do this one together at the board before you do it on your own question. Use the paper you already dissected in Week 1, because you know what is in it and can spend the time on the method instead of on the reading.

Davis, A. S. (2010). Cover-crop roller-crimper contributes to weed management in no-till soybean. Weed Science, 58(3), 300-309.

Step one: pull the indexable ideas out of that title. There are roughly four. A practice, a machine, an outcome, and a crop system.

Step two: for each, list the other words a different field, decade, or country might use for the same idea. This is the step students skip, and it is the step that decides whether your search finds the literature or a slice of it.

Notice three things about that table. Hyphens and spaces vary, so you search both forms. One row is a crop and one row is a tillage system, which means the “System” row may need to become two rows once you see how it behaves. And “rye” is in the first row even though it is a species rather than a synonym, because in this literature a rye search retrieves cover-crop work that never uses the phrase “cover crop.”

Step three: keep the table. This is your keyword database, and it is a living document. Every time a good record shows you a term you did not have, add it to the right row and note where you found it. By Week 5 the table is part of the evidence that your search was systematic rather than lucky.

Now build the same table for your own question in the Search Workbook. Four rows at most. If you cannot fill a row with at least two alternate terms, that is worth saying out loud in class: either the concept is already precise, or you do not yet know the vocabulary of the literature you are entering.

Database syntax varies, so record the exact version you actually ran rather than pretending one string works everywhere.

2. Choose the Search Environment

Choose by coverage, not by habit. A database is not a neutral window onto the literature. It is a bounded collection that somebody decided to build, and the boundary is the whole point: what a database does not index, you will not find in it, no matter how good your search string is.

That gives you a rule you can apply without knowing anything about a specific database. Ask what a collection was built to cover, then ask whether your question lives inside that coverage.

There are three kinds of tool in play this week, and they do different jobs.

Policy, economics, history, design, education, and qualitative work each have their own subject collections, and your project may need one this table does not name.

ImportantThe Two Library Routes Worth Your Time

The University Libraries help and research-guide routes are how you find out which collection covers your question. Two things there are worth more than the rest, so use these rather than browsing.

Research guides. A subject librarian has already written down which databases cover a field and which are the starting points. That is exactly the question you are trying to answer, answered by someone who does it professionally. Reading the guide for your field takes about 10 minutes and routinely saves an hour of guessing.

A research consultation. You can meet with a librarian about your specific question. This is a free, ordinary service that graduate students use constantly, and undergraduates underuse. Bring your keyword table and your decision log; a consultation with a documented failed search is far more productive than one that starts with “I need sources.”

Access runs through your University login, so a paywall you hit off-campus is usually a login problem rather than a real barrier. If a database asks for payment, stop and route it through the Libraries instead of paying.

Google Scholar deserves a specific warning rather than a general one. It is genuinely useful for retrieving a title you already know and for following a cited-by link. But it does not publish what it indexes or how it ranks, which means you cannot state its coverage, cannot reproduce today’s result set tomorrow, and cannot defend the boundary of your search. Use it as a supplement to a search you can describe, not as the search itself.

3. Keep a Decision Log

Run the first query, then inspect the result set before changing anything. Record:

  • the database and date;
  • the exact query and filters;
  • the result count;
  • two results that fit and one that does not; and
  • the single change you will make next.

If a query fails, keep it. A failed query with a diagnosed problem is evidence of a decision. A perfect-looking final string with no path into it is not.

This Is the Second Half of the Ledger You Started in Week 1

Recall from Week 1 that we filled in the Literature Intake Form together on the Davis paper, and that its last section had you write a first claims-and-evidence entry: the claim, the evidence and its location, why the evidence is relevant, a limit, and a status. That was not a one-week exercise. It was the first row of a record you keep all semester.

You are now building the other half of that record, and the two answer different questions:

Neither one substitutes for the other. A search log with no ledger tells a reader you looked, but not what you found that you can stand behind. A ledger with no search log tells a reader what you believe, but not whether you looked anywhere you might have been contradicted.

The connection is operational, not just conceptual. When a source survives the role and fit decision in step four, it does not stop at the search log. Open a ledger entry for it using the same five fields you used in Week 1. Then the citation key in your log and the citation key in your ledger are the same string, and you can move between the two records without reconstructing anything.

The forms are on the course site and stay available all semester:

  • Week 1 Literature Intake Form, including the printable two-page version, for the five ledger fields; and
  • the reading protocol for the longer procedure behind a careful entry.

Week 1 also described how to move these entries off paper once the structure is habit: fixed-heading child notes in Zotero, one row per claim in a spreadsheet, or linked source and claim tables in a database. Week 4 is a reasonable point to make that move, because this is the week your source count starts growing faster than your memory. One warning from that discussion is worth repeating now that you have Zotero open: keep one row per claim, not one row per article. A single paper routinely supports three usable claims and undercuts a fourth.

4. Evaluate Role and Fit

Do not ask only whether a source is peer reviewed. Ask what work it can do for this project.

The outcome is not a universal score. It is a bounded decision: keep for a named purpose, set aside with a reason, or inspect more closely before using.

5. Build a Zotero Library That Preserves Decisions

The University Libraries citation-management page and Zotero documentation provide current installation and troubleshooting guidance. In class:

  1. Create one collection for the thesis project.
  2. Save sources from the item page when possible, not from an isolated PDF.
  3. Check the item type, title, authors, year, journal or publisher, volume, issue, pages, and Digital Object Identifier (DOI) after capture.
  4. Use the tags pearl, backward, and forward for discovery path. Add core, supporting, or background only after deciding the source’s role.
  5. Attach a note with three headings: Main contribution, Connection to my question, and Limits or unresolved questions.

Zotero stores what you give it. A captured record with bad metadata produces a bad citation faster. Verify first.

Wednesday: Grow a Source Set

Format: Reading clinic, citation-network workshop, and partner audit.

Reading Clinic: Who Does This Paper Cite?

Bring your marked Week 1 article and the reading protocol. Choose two or three framing sentences in the introduction. For each sentence, identify the source or sources cited and name the job the citation is doing: defining the problem, establishing what is known, marking a disagreement, or justifying the study.

This manual trace is the bridge into pearl growing. You are not collecting every citation. You are following the ones that do work relevant to your question.

Pearl-Growing Workflow

  1. Choose the pearl. Find one review article published in 2020 or later that is close enough to orient your problem. If no suitable review exists, document the search and use a well-connected primary or methods source after checking with me.
  2. Trace backward. Select five cited sources that bear directly on your focused problem. Record the reason for each selection. Repeated citation is a clue, not an automatic inclusion rule.
  3. Trace forward. Use a cited-by tool in Web of Science, Scopus, or Google Scholar. Select five later sources that cite the pearl and address your problem. Record the reason for each selection.
  4. Capture and verify. Add the pearl and the 10 traced sources to Zotero. Apply the discovery-path tags and correct incomplete metadata.
  5. Annotate three. For one pearl, backward, and forward source, record the main contribution, connection to your question, and limits or unresolved questions.
  6. Audit with a partner. Ask whether another reader can reconstruct why each of the three annotated sources belongs in the set.

If a branch produces nothing useful, record that result. Change the pearl, move one concept level broader, or take the documented question to a University Libraries research consultation.
